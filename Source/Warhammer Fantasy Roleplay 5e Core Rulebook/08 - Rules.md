# • **RULES** •

*'Aye, there are times when things just go wrong. Really, badly wrong. Like that time halfway up the Bögen when Olaf jumped into a tree but missed and hit that jagged branch square on. I think he's there still, although there's probably not much of him left after the animals had a season's grazing. By Sigmar's grace, sit yourself down and have some cheese, there's nothing good for you out there!'*

— Dieter Käsegeier, Cheesemonger

These rules exist to help you explore the world of **Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay**. They describe how the Old World works, from gutter brawls and backroom intrigue to magic and the horrors of Chaos, and provide a fair way to decide what happens when the outcome is uncertain.

Roleplaying games work best with minimal interruption to the narrative flow, so bear these things in mind to help you have the best games possible:

- **To roll or not to roll.** You don't need to roll dice to determine the outcome of every action. If success or failure would have little impact on the progression of the story, the GM can simply decide what happens next.
- **Know what you're rolling.** Understanding the rules lets players judge the risks before they act. Before rolling dice, make sure that everyone involved knows what is being rolled for and the chances of success or failure.

- **Don't get bogged down.** The rules provide a framework for your adventures, but in something as free form as roleplaying they can't anticipate every situation that may arise. If you come up against a situation not covered in the rules, modify them as you see fit to reach a solution everyone agrees with. The important thing is to keep the game flowing and have fun.

# **TESTS**

At the core of the rules are Tests. Can you persuade a suspicious watchman to let you pass, or decipher the arcane inscriptions on a book that murmurs in the voice of the witch who wrote it? Routine actions and anything with an obvious outcome simply succeed, without the need for a Test. When the outcome of an action is uncertain or opposed by another character, you make a Test.

# MAKING A TEST

- Player describes what their Character is trying to do.
- GM determines if a Test is required. If it is, GM explains any factors affecting the action that would be obvious to the Character.
- If the player still wishes to proceed, the GM decides what Skill or Characteristic to Test.

# **ROLL DICE**

- Player rolls the dice, a d100, trying to get equal or lower than their Skill. If they do, they have succeeded.
- If it's important to know how well or badly they did, we use the Test's **Success Level** (SL). Subtract the first digit of your roll from the first digit of the Skill or Characteristic being tested to see how many SL you've generated, as shown below.

**CHECK YOUR SKILL OR CHARACTERISTIC**

#### **MODIFIERS AND 0 SL**

Sometimes, SL modifiers will adjust the result of a Test to 0 SL.

- If your dice roll was less than or equal to the skill being tested, the outcome is a Marginal Success (+0 SL).
- If it was higher, the outcome is a Marginal Failure (-0 SL).

### **DARKNESS AND TESTS**

If your target is in darkness, Melee Tests, Ranged Tests, and Perception Tests based on sight incur Disadvantage. Sources of illumination (see **Chapter 11: Consumer Guide**), as well as Talents and Traits such as Darkvision (page 357) may mitigate this.

#### **REPEATING TESTS**

For a Character to repeat a failed Test, they must first have dealt with the consequences of their failure, or the circumstances surrounding the Test must have substantially changed.

#### **AUTOMATIC SUCCESS AND FAILURE**

No matter the odds, hope is always present, and success is never guaranteed. A roll of 96-100 is always a failure, resulting in at least a Marginal Failure. A roll of 01-05 is always a success, achieving at least a Marginal Success.

## **CRITICALS AND FUMBLES**

Sometimes talent, luck, or an unfortunate lack of either means you do far better, or worse, than expected. When you roll a double, work out the final SL of the Test as normal:

- Success (+0 SL or more) becomes a Critical Success — treat it as Astounding (+5 SL) unless there's an even better outcome!
- Failure (-0 SL or worse) means a Fumble. Treat the result as an Astounding Failure (-5 SL) instead, unless it would otherwise be worse…

Combat Melee Tests work differently. The Critical/Fumble doesn't modify your SL, instead a Critical inflicts a Critical Wound (page 171) on your opponent, and you roll on the **Oops Table** for a Fumble. Then proceed with the results of the Test as normal.

#### **ADVANTAGE AND DISADVANTAGE**

Sometimes a rule, ability, or circumstance will grant you Advantage or Disadvantage on a Test. When making a Test with Advantage, you may reverse the digits of a roll if that would improve your result, so a 71 could become a 17. When making a Test with Disadvantage, you must reverse the digits if the result would be worse, so a 19 becomes a 91.

Advantage and Disadvantage cancel each other out. If you have multiple sources of Advantage, each one after the first grants +1 SL, while multiple sources of Disadvantage inflict -1 SL.

# **DIFFICULTY AND CHARACTER MODIFIERS**

Not all Tests are as simple as the one above. The Success Level of a Test can be modified by how difficult it is to succeed, or by the Character's abilities and equipment:

- Difficulty set by the GM, with easier tasks giving an SL bonus and harder ones giving a penalty (see **Difficulty Table**).
- Character's modifiers come from Talents, equipment, Spells, and other effects. These will be clear on the player's Character Sheet.

Combine any SL bonuses or penalties from the Difficulty with the Character's modifiers to arrive at the final SL modifier for the roll. This could turn a success into a failure, or prise victory from the jaws of defeat!

#### **DIFFICULTY TABLE**

| Difficulty  | Test Modifier | Example                                                         |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Very Easy   | +6 SL            | Noticing the obvious, jumping a small gap                    |
| Easy        | +4 SL            | Sharpening the edge of a trusty blade                           |
| Average     | +2 SL            | Quickly finding pertinent information in a book you can read |
| Challenging | +0               | Typical Ranged and Melee attack Tests, climbing a stone wall |
| Difficult   | –1 SL            | Recalling an obscure fact about a long-dead noble            |
| Hard        | –2 SL            | Swimming upstream in a flooding sewer                        |
| Very Hard   | –3 SL            | Convincing a witch hunter they've made a terrible mistake    |

If a difficulty is not specified, you should assume it is Challenging (+0 SL). Most Tests made during Combat are Challenging (+0 SL).

#### **OPPOSED TESTS**

Resisted actions, such as trying to sneak past someone or hit a foe in combat (page 164), are Opposed Tests. Whichever of you achieved the higher SL wins. You do not need to succeed on your own Test to win, just beat your opponent. The difference between your SL is the SL of the Opposed Test. For example, -1 SL beats -4 SL, winning by 3 SL — an Impressive Success.

In the case of a tie, whoever initiated the roll wins — in combat, the attacker.

#### **CHARACTERISTIC TESTS**

If an action isn't covered by a Skill, you can make a Characteristic Test instead of a Skill Test. The GM determines the most appropriate Characteristic for what you are attempting, and you Test it as normal.

# **SUMMARY & OUTCOME**

- Rolling equal to or under your Skill gives you positive SL, rolling above it gives you negative SL.
- Difficulty or Character modifiers adjust the SL
- A positive SL result means you succeed, and a negative SL result means you fail.

Often, you will only need to know whether a Test results in a success or failure. If it's important to know just how well you have passed, or how badly you have failed, consult the **Outcomes Table** below. The GM uses the descriptions in the table to decide what happens as a result of your action. There are lots of example outcome tables for a variety of actions, along with specific advice on how to use the rules, later in this chapter.

#### **EXTENDED TESTS**

Sometimes the GM may ask you to make multiple rolls over time, such as once per round, or once each day, while keeping a running total of the SL you have achieved. This is known as an Extended Test, and it is used when time and resources matter, such as repairing a battered breastplate or picking a lock before a guard arrives. An Extended Test succeeds once you have achieved a set target number of SL, and fails should you run out of time or resources. See page 266 for more on Extended Tests.

# **Test Examples**

Here are a few examples of Tests to help you get to grips with the rules.

#### **Typical Test**

Martha is playing Markus Kurtentaager, a Reiklander merchant attempting to make her way through Altdorf without attracting the attention of anyone she owes money to. She tells the GM that Markus is sticking to quiet streets, wearing a hood, and otherwise trying not to be seen — the GM rules that's an **Average (+2 SL) Stealth (Urban)** Test. Markus has Stealth (Urban) 39, and rolls a 41. That's -1 SL, as the result on the dice is higher, and 3 - 4 = -1. That's normally a failure, but adding +2 SL because the Test is of Average Difficulty makes it a success (+1 SL). The GM tells Martha that no one seems to have realised Markus is in town… yet.

#### **Criticals and Fumbles**

Confident after his earlier efforts, Markus again Tests Stealth (Urban) to make his way back to the docks and his waiting river barge. This time, he rolls a 66. Not only is this a failure, but as he also rolled a double, it's a fumble! The GM tells Martha that Markus hasn't just been spotted, he's stumbled right into the drunk and angry Kalk Grumdraagen, to whom he owes over a hundred gold crowns. Kalk recognises Markus on the spot, and lays one hand on the haft of his axe as his pockmarked face widens into a grim smile.

During the ensuing tussle, Markus makes attempts to race down the slick boards of the dock and onto his barge. The GM rules this is a **Difficult (-1 SL) Athletics** Test, as the slick boards make such acrobatics difficult. Markus's Athletics is 30, but Martha rolls an 11 — not only a success, but a Critical! The GM rules, at Martha's suggestion, that not only has Markus made the leap, but he's managed to unmoor the boat and push off as well, safely on the water before Kalk can reach him.

Criticals and Fumbles work a little differently when making Melee or Ranged Tests in combat — see page 165.

#### **Rolling with Advantage**

Molli ducks into an alley to hide from some approaching guards. Her Alley Cat Talent lets her make Stealth (Urban) Test with Advantage. Molli's Stealth (Urban) Skill is 46 and she rolls a 72. Normally this would be a failure, but Advantage allows her to reverse the numbers for a result of 27 — a success!

Disadvantage works the same way, but you must reverse the results if they would be worse. So if Molli suffered from Disadvantage on a Test, then a roll of 18 would be reversed to result in an 81 instead.

#### **Opposed Tests**

Salundra attempts to strike down a rampaging orc in the midst of battle. As the orc is directly trying to stop her by parrying the blow, the roll is opposed. Salundra makes a **Challenging (+0 SL) Melee (Basic)** Test. Her Skill is 55, and she rolls a 23, scoring +3 SL. The orc is no pushover however, and manages to score +2 SL. Salundra wins the Test, but only scores +1 SL (3 - 2 = 1). She hits the orc, adding +1 to her usual Damage (see page 164 for how to calculate Damage).

Incensed, the orc swings at Salundra on its turn. He rolls -2 SL, but Salundra scores a far worse -4 SL! Despite rolling more than his skill, the orc still wins the opposed Test with +2 SL (2-4 = 2). The blow is awkwardly made, but it connects, leaving Salundra injured and looking for a way out…

#### **Getting Help**

With the GM's permission, two or more characters can work together on a Test. The character with the highest Skill (or Characteristic) makes the Test with Advantage.

To help, a character must logically be able to meaningfully assist. Characters cannot normally help resist disease, poison, fear, hazards, or physically assist if they aren't close enough. A Character cannot assist with an Advanced Skill Test unless they have at least one Advance in the Skill being tested.

*Example*: *Brokk and Salundra are trying to force open the barred door to a noble's cellar. The GM calls for a Challenging (+0 SL) Strength Test. Brokk has the higher Strength rating, at 53, so he makes the Test. He rolls a 61, but with Salundra helping he has Advantage. This allows him to reverse the roll to a 16, resulting in a +3 SL. Together they force the door open, and descend the darkened stairs with care.*

Whether it is guts, luck, or the favour of the gods, Fate and Fortune smile upon you. You can spend them to gain an edge or cheat unpleasant consequences.

#### **Spend a Fortune Point to:**

- Gain Advantage on a Test before rolling.
- Reroll a Test, keeping the new result. You may spend another Fortune to reroll again.
- Remove one Condition (page 184).

#### **Replenishing Fortune**

You regain all Fortune Points at the start of each gaming session. Certain encounters, Spells, and abilities may also replenish (or remove!) Fortune Points.

#### **Fate**

Fate points are far more precious than Fortune. They mark you as a Character of destiny, allowing you to Cheat Death or Achieve the Impossible.

#### **Cheating Death**

You may spend a Fate Point to delay your appointment with Morr in the following ways:

- **How Did That Miss?** After any dice are rolled for an attack against you, but before Damage is calculated, Opposed Test outcomes resolved or Critical Hits are determined, spend a Fate Point to negate the effects of that attack. The blow misses, the spell fizzles, or you otherwise escape by the skin of your teeth. Play then continues as normal.
- **Not Today!** Instead of dying, your Character is knocked unconscious, left for dead, swept away by a river, or otherwise removed from the encounter. Whatever the circumstances, your Character survives, but takes no further part in the current encounter.

#### **Achieving the Impossible**

You may spend a Fate Point to succeed where failure seems certain.

- **I Will Not Fail!** Instead of rolling a Test, choose the result instead. You automatically succeed, even in the direst circumstances. In an Opposed Test, you win by at least +1 SL. If you choose to score a Critical, you may pick the Hit Location instead of rolling randomly.

#### **Replenishing Fate**

**FATE AND FORTUNE** V Fate is not automatically replenished. The GM may grant a Fate Point for an act of exceptional heroism, bravery, or significance. This usually happens only at the end of an important adventure, so spend Fate carefully — it is rarely replenished.

# **THEFT AND SKULLDUGGERY**

Crime is common in the Empire. Some say anything not nailed down is likely to be stolen, but that overlooks the thriving second-hand market for questionably acquired nails.

Characters are likely to break the law from time to time, often for good reason. A crime may even be the focus of an adventure, such as breaking into a wealthy merchant's home to uncover evidence of cult activity. In such cases, every detail matters, from breaking in and searching for hidden evidence to escaping unseen.

There is often no need to make a Test when time is not an issue or failure has few consequences. For example, if the players steal a locked chest, you can simply say they force it open once they have time, rather than requiring a Pick Lock Test.

#### **A TIME FOR CRIME**

If a Character earns their living through crime, such as a Thief or Charlatan, treat it like any other Career. Their routine work is resolved during Downtime using the *Income* Endeavour.

There is no need to roleplay picking the pocket of every NPC they encounter — that would quickly become frustrating for the other players!

# **Stealth and Surveillance**

Moving unseen and spotting danger are valuable skills throughout the Empire. Here are some common situations and how to resolve them.

#### **Keeping an Eye Out**

If a Character is watching for threats or suspects they are being followed, make an Opposed Perception Test against the target's appropriate Stealth Skill. The GM should allow the Test even if no one is there — a little paranoia is often healthy.

#### **Casing the Joint**

To observe a location, make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Perception**  Test. For each +SL, the GM reveals one useful fact, such as who comes and goes, how well the location is guarded, what lies inside, ways to enter or leave unseen, or whether anyone else is watching it.

#### **Sneaking Around**

To move unseen, make the appropriate Stealth Test (Rural, Underground, or Urban) opposed by the Perception of whoever you are trying to avoid. If there are multiple observers, use the highest Perception.

A suitable distraction, such as a modest fire, a street brawl, or a blathering guest (page 115), grants Advantage on Stealth Tests.

| COMMON FORMS OF SKULLDUGGERY | |
|---|---|
| Action | Test |
| Pick a pocket | A Sleight of Hand Test, opposed by your target's Perception. |
| Force open a door or window | A Strength Test to do it quickly, or a Stealth (Urban) Test with the right tools to do it quietly. |
| Pick a lock | A Pick Lock Test, with a difficulty set by the lock's quality. Especially challenging locks might require an Extended Test, with one roll every round, or every minute. |
| Connect with local criminals | A Difficult (-1 SL) Gossip Test, made with Advantage if you have the Criminal Talent yourself. |
| Argue your innocence | A Charm Test, opposed by your opponent's Cool, or an opposed Lore (Law) Test to do so in court. Either way, Test with Disadvantage if you were caught red-handed or if there is incriminating evidence. |
| Cheat at cards | A Sleight of Hand Test, opposed by your opponent's Perception. Success grants Advantage on your next Gamble Test during that game. |
| Bribe a guard | A Bribery Test to find someone open to being bribed, at a price set by their status. You might Haggle with them over the price. |
| Learn a thing's value | An Evaluate Test to learn something's value, or a Difficult (-1 SL) Evaluate Test to quickly spot the most valuable thing in a room. |

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Stealth (Rural) Test to infiltrate a guarded manor house.**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** You sneak inside and happen upon a servant's livery drying on the line — the perfect disguise should you wish to explore the living quarters.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** You sneak inside and notice a poorly guarded garden path you can later use to make a quick escape.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You sneak inside the manor without being seen.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You sneak inside, but leave signs of your entrance that will surely be noticed come morning.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You fail to find a safe way inside, but manage to avoid being spotted by the guards during your attempt.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You're discovered by a guard who spots you from across the courtyard. Time to run!

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** You stumble into the path of a patrol, who bellow a warning to the estate before drawing the blades and bearing down on you.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** A misplaced step sees you tumbling from a watch house roof and right into the path of the captain of the guards, who recognises you on the spot.

#### **Silent Takedowns**

To silently subdue an unaware guard or sentry, make an Opposed Melee Test against the target's Endurance. If the attacker wins, they inflict damage equal to their Strength Bonus and the target gains the *Unconscious* Condition.

If the target wins, they instead gain the *Stunned* Condition and immediately raise the alarm.

Characters with the *Strike to Stun* Talent gain Advantage on this Test. Silent Takedowns can only be attempted outside combat against unaware targets.

# **Gambling and Cheating**

To resolve a game of chance, make an Opposed Gamble Test. The character with the highest SL wins.

To cheat, make a Sleight of Hand Test opposed by the highest Perception among your opponents. Success grants Advantage on your next Gamble Test in that game. Failure usually means you are caught.

Use these rules only when the outcome of the game is important to the adventure. Routine gambling for income is best handled with the *Income* Endeavour (page 199).

# **Traps**

From simple tripwires to pits filled with spikes, traps are laid across the Old World to hunt animals and deter unwanted interlopers. Encounters with traps may run from the mundane need to snare a brace of rabbits for dinner, to dealing with an ancient mechanism constructed to guard a Nehekharan tomb.

#### **Setting a Trap**

Setting and disarming simple snares while hunting for food is handled as part of an Outdoor Survival Test. Setting a trap to ensnare a specific target is done with a Set Trap Test, with a Difficulty determined by the complexity of the trap in question — see below. Failing a Set Trap Test is not usually dangerous, but if a Set Trap Test is Fumbled, there may be serious consequences as listed in the **Selection of Traps** table.

Unless specified otherwise, setting a trap takes about 20 minutes.

## **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

#### **A Sleight of Hand Test to cheat at cards.**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** You grant yourself enough cards to win any game you wish without making a Gamble Test at all. You could even frame another character for cheating, if you like!

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** You pocket enough high cards to grant yourself Advantage on any Gamble Tests you make during the night's merriment.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You pocket a pair of useful cards, granting you Advantage on your next Gamble Test.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You gain Advantage on your next Gamble Test, but one of your opponents grows suspicious of you.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You pass off a failed palming of a card as mere clumsiness. Your opponents are all suspicious of you and watch you carefully from now on.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You are caught cheating by at least two of your opponents, who immediately call for your winnings to be confiscated, and you are kicked out into the frigid night.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** Your cheating is obvious for all to see, and your opponents offer you two ways out — a night in the stocks, or a duel on the morrow.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** A mistimed flourish sees pocketed cards spill from your coat for all to see. The bar erupts in an uproar, and a fight breaks out over who gets to gut you first.

#### **Spotting a Trap**

Spotting a trap requires a Perception Test. The Difficulty is determined by the result of the Set Trap Test made to deploy the mechanism.

If a Character specifically checks a small area, such as a door or hallway, for a trap, they gain Advantage on the associated Perception Test.

#### **Triggering a Trap**

If a Character fails to spot a trap and moves through the area it protects, the trap is triggered. Some traps may allow a Dodge Test to avoid any ill effects, but typically the unfortunate victim simply suffers consequences. In addition to any other effects, a Character affected by a trap also suffers the *Surprised* Condition.

#### **Disarming a Trap**

Once a Trap has been spotted, Disarming it requires a Set Trap Test with a Difficulty one step higher than that required to deploy it. You have Advantage on Tests to disarm a trap you set.

# **Breaking and Entering**

Characters will often want something others don't want them to have, or need to get somewhere they shouldn't. Locks, barred doors, and similar obstacles may deter casual opportunists, but rarely stop a determined intruder.

#### **Picking Locks**

Opening a lock without its key requires lockpicks (page 310). Improvised tools, such as a hairpin or nail, impose Disadvantage.

Picking a lock is an Extended Test (page 131), with one Test allowed each round. The lock determines the Difficulty and SL required.

If time is not an issue and discovery is unlikely, a character with the Pick Lock Skill and suitable tools can simply open the lock without making a Test.

With appropriate tools, such as a crowbar (page 310), they can instead attempt a quiet entry with a **Challenging (+0 SL) Stealth** Test, opposed by an observer's Perception. Particularly sturdy barriers may increase the Difficulty.

# **Secret Signs**

Many organisations use coded signs to mark opportunities, warn of danger, or claim territory. Thieves, spies, and cultists often scratch subtle symbols onto doorways, signposts, or even coins to communicate with one another.

Make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Secret Signs (Thief )** Test to spot these signs, or to leave a message for others who understand the same signs.

# **Theft and Scams**

Relieving others of their belongings has always been a popular pursuit, and the Characters may find themselves taking part from time to time.

#### **Mugging**

Intimidating a victim into handing over their valuables requires an Intimidate Test opposed by the target's Cool. The Difficulty depends on the circumstances — a lone traveller on a forest road is far easier to intimidate than three stevedores on a busy Altdorf street. Escaping before the authorities arrive is usually a Stealth or Athletics Test.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Secret Signs Test to gather information on local thieves**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** As below, and you find subtle signs designed to misdirect most, but which point to another thief 's cache. It contains a pouch of 1d10 Silver Shillings nestled between a crowbar and a length of rope.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** As below, and you also discover which streets are the most carefully patrolled by the town watch.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You spot some signs pointing to a local bar frequented by the criminal underworld, where you can doubtless learn more.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You spot signs indicating the way to a local bar, but draw undue attention from someone else — a wary burgher, or perhaps a fellow thief.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You fail to spot any hidden signs, but a local vagabond notices what you're doing. Laughing, he points you in the right direction, but spreads word that you're somewhat of a fool.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You spot nothing, but avoid drawing undue attention to yourself.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** You misread a sign and end up in a bar frequented by the town watch.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** You completely misread some signs and find yourself stumbling into the den of the most notorious and least welcoming gang boss in town.

#### **Picking Pockets**

Picking a pocket requires a Sleight of Hand Test opposed by the victim's Perception. A Test is only required when the theft is important to the adventure, such as stealing a jailer's keys. Routine theft is best handled with the *Income* Endeavour during Downtime.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Charm Test to convince a merchant to invest their coin with you**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** The merchant invests ten gold crowns in your scheme and convinces an even more credulous business partner to invest ten more.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** The merchant is so impressed by your scheme that he immediately places ten gold crowns with you.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** The merchant happily parts with the five gold crowns you sought.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You convince your mark to invest a gold crown, but he'd like to invite his brother into the opportunity as well, who just so happens to be an officer in the town watch.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You fail to secure the coin, but the merchant isn't aware you were trying to con him.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** The merchant passes on your investment and advises colleagues to avoid you.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** The merchant sees right through your scam and reports you to the town watch.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** The merchant avoids your obvious scam, but recognises your enthusiasm, if not your talent. He offers you a deal — help him con his partner, or he will report you to the city watch himself.

#### **Criminal Coinage**

Counterfeit currency is common, often involving coating a base metal, such as lead, in a thin layer of gold or silver. Real currency is sometimes debased by clipping, removing a small amount of precious metal from the edges. Merchants are on the lookout for such things, and a successful Evaluate Test reveals if a payment contains any obviously dodgy currency.

> When forging coinage, a **Hard (-2 SL) Art (Engraving) Test** is made to create a convincing die, followed by a Trade (Smith) Test to strike the coins. Unless relevant to the events of an adventure, this is best handled via the *Income* Endeavour (page 199).

#### **Fraud and Charlatanry**

Routine scams, such as selling dubious cures, are almost indistinguishable from more honest forms of commerce and usually resolved during Downtime, unless it is relevant to the adventure.

More ambitious cons require a Charm Test opposed by the victim's Cool, with the Difficulty determined by how believable the deception is. Selling a fake cure to a desperate victim might be Average (+2 SL), while persuading a canny merchant to invest 100 Gold Crowns in a dubious venture in distant Lustria could be Very Hard (-3 SL).

# **FLATTERY, BRIBERY, AND STATUS**

Conversations between Characters and NPCs are at the heart of **WFRP**. If a Character says the right thing, or asks for something an NPC has no reason to refuse, there is usually no need for a Test. Asking a bystander about recent events or a merchant the price of their wares, for example, simply works.

A Test is only needed when the request involves risk, effort, or persuasion, or when gathering valuable or dangerous information (see **Asking Around**, page 150).

#### **Difficulty Factors of Social Tests**

Use the following table as a guide when setting the Difficulty of a Social Test. Positive factors make a Test easier, while negative factors make it harder. These are only guidelines. The GM has the final say, and some NPCs will simply never agree to a Character's demands, no matter how charming or intimidating they may be.

Add up the positive factors, and subtract the negative. If you are left with a positive number, the Test should be Average (+2 SL) or Easy (+4 SL). If you are left with a negative number, the Test should be Difficult (-1 SL), Hard (-2 SL), or Very Hard (-3 SL).

# **Status and Social Standing**

Old World society is rigidly stratified. Commoners know their place, while the elite enjoy power and privilege that are rarely questioned. Everyone else is acutely aware of their social standing, and many spend their lives trying to improve it.

Your Status is determined by your current Career level:

- **Gold:** The rulers of society, their advisers, protectors, magistrates, and the independently wealthy. Reaching Gold usually requires talent, success, and more than a little luck.
- **Silver:** Skilled professionals, merchants, artisans, and respected tradesfolk. Though not wealthy or powerful, they enjoy a comfortable and respectable place in society.
- **Brass:** Labourers, peasants, unskilled workers, and the poor. This Tier also includes many criminals, ne'er-do-wells, and those with no steady livelihood.

The number after your Status is your Standing, your social position relative to others of your Status.

# **Maintaining Status**

Your Status can fall if you fail to look the part. If you lack the Trappings of your current Career level, most people treat you as having the Status of the highest Career level whose Trappings you possess. After all, is an Officer without a uniform, regiment, or symbol of rank really worthy of much respect?

# **Changing Status**

Your Status usually changes only when you change Career, advance to a new Career level, or lose the Trappings needed to maintain it. The GM may, however, also adjust your Status to reflect important events in the game. A Merchant with the graf 's ear might temporarily gain Status, while a Noble who loses their estate and falls from favour might see theirs reduced.

# **Status and Social Tests**

Status shapes almost every social interaction in the Empire. Those of higher Status are used to giving orders, while those beneath them are expected to know their place. Crossing these social boundaries often makes Social Tests more difficult. A noble may happily enjoy a Brass Status Entertainer's performance, but be far less willing to share a drink afterwards.

Status also affects how much you earn from the *Income* Endeavour (page 199).

**Charm and Fellowship Tests:** Charm, Gossip, and most other Fellowship Tests suffer -1 SL for each tier of Status between the characters. A Silver Status merchant incurs -1 SL when dealing with a Brass Status peasant, while that same peasant suffers -2 SL when dealing with a Gold Status noble.

**Intimidate Tests:** While within civilisation, Gold Status characters gain Advantage when intimidating those of lower Status. In the wilds, or once violence has already begun, this benefit does not apply.

**Leadership:** Status plays a very important role in Leadership. Indeed, the State Army normally only grants higher ranks to those of noble birth. Characters gain Advantage on Leadership Tests made to command those of lower Status.

**Standing:** Standing rarely has a mechanical effect, but characters usually defer to others of the same Status with a higher Standing.

## **Making Friends (and Enemies)**

It's often not what you know, but who you know. Winning over the right people can earn you money, information, or powerful allies.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Charm Test to make a good impression at a courtly ball.**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** You are the life of the party, your wit and good graces a highlight of the evening for many. You depart having been introduced to many noteworthy individuals, and with an invitation to at least one private dinner.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** You seamlessly fit in with those present, joining conversations as though you were an old friend. Many wonder why they haven't seen you at such events before.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You fit in well, receiving a few mostly sincere invitations to future events, and inviting polite inquiries into your own interests and agenda.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You acquit yourself well, recalling an anecdote that saw a polite chuckle cross the room. Most view you as at least somewhat interesting.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You fade into the general patter of the court, unable to make yourself seen, but at least learning the names of some important individuals you might pursue directly.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** Your clumsy attempts at inserting yourself into the court's good graces fall flat. Most barely remember you were there at all.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** You embarrass yourself terribly, spilling wine on both yourself and a lady of the court. You come off as something of a fool, unworthy of time or attention.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** A careless word or scathing insult leaves many present aghast at your indiscretion. You earn a foul reputation with at least one NPC or faction present.

#### **Make a Good Impression**

Making a good impression usually requires a Charm Test, though the player should describe or roleplay what their Character says or does. The Difficulty depends on factors such as the Character's reputation, recent actions, appearance, Status, and affiliations. A well-dressed diplomat with a generous purse is likely to receive a warmer welcome than a ragged Flagellant preaching doom.

#### **Befriend Someone Useful**

Building a useful relationship usually happens naturally through roleplay, as characters get to know one another and exchange favours. If you need a contact quickly, however, you may spend some time and make a Charm Test to befriend a useful NPC, such as a member of the town watch, a successful merchant, or a well-connected criminal. On a success, you gain Advantage on future Charm Tests with that NPC. Useful contacts expect favours in return, and are unlikely to remain friendly if you let them down.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**An Intimidate Test to convince a merchant to close up shop**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** The merchant is terrified of you, and departs the town that very night, perhaps leaving behind several valuables in their haste to get away from you.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** The merchant, certain their life is on the line, asks for a few days to get their affairs in order, but otherwise agrees to your demands.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** The merchant agrees, at least for now, and closes their premises.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** The merchant takes your threats seriously, but attempts to extort you for a substantial sum of money before they will consider closing their store.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** The merchant does not think your threats are credible, but offers you a pittance of a bribe to simply be on your way.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** The merchant assumes you are simply trying to shake them down, and offers a small bribe, while intenting to spell a larger sum on hiring some protection.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** The merchant laughs, draws a weapon, and orders you out into the street while calling loudly for the guards.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** Your half-hearted threats are seen as rank weakness, and the merchant laughs in your face, or offers to duel you right now and settle the matter outright.

#### **SUCCESS AND CONSEQUENCE**

Charm, Intimidate, and similar Skills are not mind control. A successful Test can make someone more helpful, compliant, or fearful, but most people will not risk everything for a stranger. Success also has consequences. A convincing lie falls apart when the truth comes to light, and those bullied or intimidated into helping you may later seek revenge — or simply inform the authorities.

# **Getting Your Way**

Most people in the Old World are out for themselves. Even so, a little flattery, a convincing argument, or an implied promise can often convince them to do what you want.

#### **Charming Others**

To persuade someone to do something they would not otherwise do, make a Charm Test. If the request is reasonable or the target is well disposed towards you, the Test is unopposed.

If you are deceiving them or asking for something unusual, they may oppose the Test with Intuition.

The Difficulty depends on what you are asking. Consider how inconvenient, dangerous, or illegal the request is, how well the target knows you, and the consequences of agreeing. Illegal requests should always be opposed and are at least Difficult (-1 SL). Asking someone to risk their life is usually Very Hard (-3 SL). Use Leadership instead of Charm when commanding willing followers, such as soldiers in battle.

On a success, you get your way — within reason. The GM is the final arbitrator of what an NPC is willing to agree to, regardless of the result.

#### **Intimidation Tactics**

When honeyed words fail, a threat can often succeed. To get your way, or simply instill Fear (page 183), describe your threat, then make an Intimidate Test opposed by the target's Cool. If you succeed, the target believes you and acts accordingly. You will get your way — for now — but the character is likely to become a future enemy.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Charm Test to convince a noble to support your claim to a mine**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** The noble is entirely taken in by your talk of injustice, and sees enough benefit to their reputation should they aid your cause that they seek nothing in return.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** The noble is impressed by your daring in attempting to enlist their aid, and supports your claim, though they make it clear you owe them a favour.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** The noble agrees to support your claim, so long as they receive a stipend from the mine once its operation is returned to you.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** The noble agrees that your claim seems just and legal, but pointedly wonders aloud what could possibly be in it for them…

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** The noble finds your petition tedious and casually mentions that they are far too preoccupied with a rival's machinations to bother with yours. Of course, if you could somehow help them with that matter, they might reconsider…

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** The noble has no interest in your foolish errand and refuses to lend you any aid.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** The noble is offended by your pathetic attempts at intrigue and actively opposes your claim when the matter next arises at court.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** The noble is insulted you'd try to draw them into your petty squabble, and vows to claim the mine for themselves.

#### **Negotiating a Discount**

Most purchases are resolved by paying the prices listed in **Chapter 11: Consumer Guide**. Characters wishing to haggle can instead make an **Opposed Challenging (+0 SL) Haggle Test** against the seller. On a success, the total price is reduced by 10%, or 20% with an Astounding Success (+6 SL). Only one Haggle Test can be made per vendor, as most merchants have little patience for negotiating every individual item.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Hard (-2 SL) Gossip Test to spread word that an unpleasant but quite mundane merchant is actually a vampire**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** The merchant is dragged off in silver chains by a witch hunter and, by the time they have proven themselves innocent, is left without a shilling to their name.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** Cruel or fearful glances follow the merchant everywhere, and their business suffers. They are jostled or even attacked in the streets, and at least consider leaving town.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** Rumours abound in high and low places of the merchant's crimes. An enterprising individual could likely rally an angry mob — see **Public Speaking**, page 145.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** The rumour is widely spread but only rarely believed. Still, unkind words and unpleasant japes dog the merchant for months.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** Most find the idea too ridiculous to be believed, but at least one gullible soul takes up your tale and shares it with any other fool they should happen across.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** The rumour never takes hold.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** The rumour is not believed, and people think less of you for trying to spread such fearmongering tales about an innocent man.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** The rumour is not believed, and word gets back to the merchant of just who has been attempting to smear their name.

#### **Gossip and Rumours**

Gossip can be used to spread rumours as well as gather information (page 146). The Difficulty depends on how interesting and believable the rumour is — stories that reinforce existing beliefs work best of all. A good rumour can travel the length of the River Reik faster than any barge. It can sway public opinion, draw a rival into the open, or tarnish a hated foe's reputation.

# **Carousing**

Coaching houses, squalid inns, and dwarf drinking halls are where many in the Old World go to forget their troubles. Business is struck, friendships forged, and rivalries begun over a tankard or three. Keeping up with your drinking companions is often a crucial component of success.

When carousing, make a Consume Alcohol Test, with the Difficulty determined by the strength of the drink. On a failure, you gain the *Fatigued* Condition. If you keep drinking, or suffer an Astounding Failure (-5 SL), you become Stinking Drunk. Roll on the following table to see what happens.

These effects last for two hours after you cease drinking, at which time they end, and you become hungover, suffering a *Fatigued*  Condition that lasts 12 - TB (Toughness Bonus) hours.

**A Hard (-2 SL) Charm Test to convince a gathered crowd to support your appointment as village alderman.**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** You are lifted aloft by the exultant crowd and paraded through the streets, with most convinced there could be no better candidate for the position.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** Most are convinced by your words, and a few are truly passionate, telling their friends and acquaintances to support you.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You make a good impression, leaving most convinced you'd make a decent alderman.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** People come away generally favourable towards your bid for the position, but could be easily swayed towards someone more compelling.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** A few people seem to nod on occasion, but no one leaves convinced you'd be suitable.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You fail to convince almost anyone that you would be a suitable alderman.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** Most people leave convinced that almost anyone other than you would be preferable.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** Rotten fruit, clumps of dirt, and anything else people can lay their hands on are tossed your way as you completely botch your attempt to curry favour.

# **Public Speaking**

V **STINKING DRUNK** Whether stirring up an audience or persuading an angry mob not to hand you over to a Witch Hunter, public speaking can be a powerful tool.

> Use Entertain (Acting) to garner the attention of a crowd, while Charm can persuade them to act. Crowds respond best to simple, direct messages, such as *'Burn down the tavern!'* or *'Do not trust the false prophet!'* The *Public Speaking* Talent lets you convey more complex ideas and use other appropriate Skills, such as Lore (Theology), instead of Charm. Gossip (page 112) can achieve similar results, though more slowly and with less control.

# **Lies and Deception**

Lies are a common tool of criminals, politicians, and anyone trying to stay out of trouble.

If someone has reason to suspect a lie, the liar makes a Charm Test opposed by the listener's Intuition. If the liar fails, the listener knows they are being deceived, but not necessarily what the truth is.

If there is no reason to suspect deception, no Test is required. This applies to lies told by NPCs as well — the GM should only call for a Test when a Character questions what they are being told.

### **Mistaken Identity**

Few people in the Empire have any reliable way to prove who they are. Most are taken at their word, especially if they have the clothing, tools, and manner expected of their profession. Even official documents, such as guild seals or wizard's licences, can be forged.

To pretend to be someone you are not, make an Entertain (Acting) Test opposed by the target's Intuition. On a success, the deception is believed, and no further Test is needed unless you give your audience reason to become suspicious.

Claiming a higher Status, or lacking the clothing and Trappings your assumed identity would be expected to have, may increase the Difficulty to Difficult (-1 SL) or higher.

Impersonating a specific individual is far more difficult. Unless nobody present knows what they look like, or you bear an extraordinary resemblance, the Difficulty should be increased, even with a convincing disguise. Some disguises, such as a halfling attempting to impersonate an adult human, are simply impossible.

# **NOSING AROUND**

The Empire is rife with secretive schemes and dark conspiracies. Nobles expend a hundred lives to move a border two fields over, while the servants of the Dark Gods work to undermine civilisation itself.

Characters will inevitably find themselves uncovering these plots — examining the site of a grisly murder, poring over dusty records, following whispered rumours, and prising secrets from those who would rather keep silent.

#### **HIDING CLUES**

Don't hide vital clues behind a Test. It can be tempting to make Players work for information, but if finding that information is essential to the adventure, they should find it.

Instead, allow Characters to notice one or two important clues straight away. Use Perception or Intuition Tests to reveal additional clues, useful insights, or a clearer understanding of what has already been discovered.

Let the Players piece the evidence together where possible, and if they still become stuck, provide another lead. If all else fails, a helpful NPC can always point the way. An unsolved mystery is intriguing — one the Characters never discover is simply frustrating.

#### **Lore**

A Character's Lore Specialisations represent their knowledge. If you have at least one Advance in an appropriate Lore, you automatically know basic facts about that subject. For example, a Character with Lore (Reikland) simply knows that the Reikland is a province of the Empire. Make a Lore Test only to recall unusual, obscure, or hidden information.

The more specific a Lore skill is to the question at hand, the more nuanced and detailed the information learned. For example, an **Average (+2 SL) Lore (Empire)** Test is sufficient to learn exactly where Helmgart is, while an **Average (+2 SL) Lore (Helmgart) Test** would reveal where it is, who rules it, the names of some prominent individuals there, and so on.

Lore represents theoretical knowledge about a subject, while Trade represents putting that knowledge into practice.

#### **Language**

Every Character speaks their native language fluently and does not need a Language Skill to use it. A Language Test is required only to convey especially complex ideas, understand obscure vocabulary or dialects, or pass as a native speaker. Once you have 4 Advances (+20) in a Language Specialisation, you speak that language fluently and no longer need to make Language Tests in most cases.

#### **Language Specialisations**

Some languages belong to the same family. Native speakers of one can usually hold a basic conversation with speakers of a related language, even without the appropriate Language Skill. They cannot read or write it, pass as a native speaker, or reliably understand complex ideas or unfamiliar vocabulary until they learn the Skill.

### **BATTLE TONGUE**

Battle Tongue is a specialised language that conveys complex tactical information in just a few words. A Character who passes a **Challenging (+0 SL) Language (Battle)** Test can use their Action to communicate up to 40 words of orders or tactical advice to others who know Language (Battle).

If players spend too long discussing tactics during combat, the GM can remind them that only Characters with Battle Tongue can exchange detailed plans in the heat of battle.

# **Research**

Most people in the Empire cannot read or write, so knowledge is usually spread by rumour, gossip, and proclamation. For the literate, however, books, records, and grimoires hold a wealth of information.

A Character with at least one Advance in Research can, given enough time, find straightforward information in a book or well-organised library without making a Test. Make a Research Test only when the information is obscure, or the source is poorly organised, incomplete, or difficult to understand.

If you do not even know where to begin, a **Challenging (+0 SL) Research Test** points you towards a useful source.

# **Asking Around**

People in the Empire are often wary of strangers, but everyone enjoys a good story. News of distant places, a round of drinks, or a few well-placed coins can loosen even the tightest lips.

#### **Good Roleplay**

Good and entertaining roleplay should be rewarded with useful information whenever appropriate, without requiring a Test. If the information is especially valuable or dangerous, a **Challenging (+0 SL) Charm Test** or a suitable bribe may be needed instead.

Players who prefer to rely on their Character's abilities rather than acting in character should still explain what they are trying to say or ask before making a Test. The GM can then decide whether a Test is required, and if so, which Skill and Difficulty are appropriate..

#### **Gossip**

A successful Gossip Test uncovers local rumours. The Difficulty depends on how willing people are to talk and where you find them. Swapping stories in a tavern might be Easy (+4 SL) or Average (+2 SL), while chatting with the guards outside your cell could be Difficult (-1 SL) or harder. Spreading a rumour is usually at least Difficult (-1 SL), depending on its target and credibility.

Rumours are not always true, but they should always be useful. Every vicious rumour about a hated noble is unlikely to be true, but the existence of such tales reveals that he is indeed hated.

#### **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**An Average (+2 SL) Gossip Test to gather rumours about a nearby mine**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** All of the below, along with tales of strange folk descending into the mine each Gheimisnacht for reasons better left unlearned.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** You learn the below, plus the name of one of the former miners who still lives nearby.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** You learn that the mine was closed after a series of unusual deaths that saw labourers refuse to enter it.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You learn that the mine closed a decade ago, but not why.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You learn nothing about the mine, but do notice that people seem reticent to talk about it, as though it is ill-omened in some way.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You learn nothing of use.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** Rumours begin to circulate that you're a wealthy merchant interested in local mines, leading to several distracting and unhelpful inquiries from local labourers and land owners.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** You waste the night drinking with a man who claims to know everything there is to know about the mine, only to learn that he was talking about a quarry three days' walk towards Altdorf.

#### **Interrogation**

To extract information from a specific individual, make a Charm or Intimidate Test opposed by the target's Cool. On a success, the target reveals something useful, whether by answering truthfully, telling an obvious lie, or giving themselves away through a notable omission.

# **Investigating**

Investigation is about interpreting clues, not finding them. Essential clues should never depend on a successful Test (see **Hiding Clues**, page 146), though careful investigation can uncover additional evidence and valuable insights.

#### **Searching a Room**

Searching a room usually requires a **Challenging (+0 SL) Perception Test**. Higher SL reveal better-hidden clues or provide additional insights. If several Characters search the same area, try to reward every successful Test with something useful, rather than letting the highest result reveal everything.

# V **EXAMPLE OUTCOMES**

**A Challenging (+0 SL) Perception Test to search the scene of a murder**

**Astounding Success (+5 SL):** All of the below, and you spot flecks of unusual purple paint or dye beneath the fingernails — a rare substance available in very few places.

**Impressive Success (+3 to +4 SL):** As below, but you also notice two indents on the victim's nose — a sign that they wore spectacles, and were likely a learned man of some kind.

**Success (+1 to +2 SL):** As below, and you additionally notice that there is not enough blood to account for how deep the wounds are — he was almost certainly killed elsewhere, then moved here.

**Marginal Success (+0 SL):** You spot the body and notice that it appears to have been moved.

**Marginal Failure (-0 SL):** You spot the dead body and notice there is something odd in how it lies, but can't say how.

**Failure (-1 to -2 SL):** You notice the body, but learn nothing of use.

**Impressive Failure (-3 to -4 SL):** You notice the dead body, but become convinced that some irrelevant detail, such as a discarded cup, may be important.

**Astounding Failure (-5 SL):** You notice the dead body, but manage to leave bloody footprints throughout the room, muddying the scene and leaving evidence of your own presence in the process.

#### **Intuition and Insights**

An Intuition Test can be used to detect a lie (page 145) or gain useful insights about another person, such as their Status (page 140), profession, origins, or general mood. An appropriate Lore or Trade Skill may be used instead, and may even grant an easier Difficulty. For example, an **Easy (+4 SL) Trade (Cook) Test** is enough to realise that a shifty-looking chef has never held a filleting knife before.

Intuition Tests can also be used to connect clues and draw conclusions from information the Characters have already uncovered. If the Players reach the correct conclusion themselves, the GM should let them know that their conjecture makes sense without requiring a Test.

## **Tracking**

Use Track to follow a trail across the wilderness. If the trail is obvious — such as a bleeding victim or footprints in fresh snow — no Test is required.

Tracking over long distances is an Extended Track Test, with one Test every few hours or once per day, depending on the pace of the pursuit. If the quarry is actively fleeing, the GM may instead use the Pursuit rules (page 158).

# **LIFE BEYOND THE WALLS**

Beyond the walls of the cities and towns of the Empire, the landscape is anything but welcoming. Aside from a few hardy farmers, daring merchants, and cautious Roadwardens, travellers are unlikely to meet a friendly face among the bandits and beastmen that trouble them. Occasional relief can be had at a price from Coaching Houses along the Empire's highways, but otherwise Characters must fend for themselves.

#### **Navigation**

Depending on the distance to be covered and the circumstances at hand, trekking through the wilds could be as simple as making a single roll before arriving at the intended destination, or the journey may take up an entire session by itself. A single Navigation Test is sufficient for each day's travel, with a Difficulty determined by the weather conditions, whether or not there is a road or river to follow, and if the Characters have a map or are otherwise familiar with the region. Success means that day's travel concludes without much incident, while failure implies as many challenges and complications as the GM wishes to impose — some advice is provided in **Chapter 9: GM's Guide**.

#### **Foraging**

While travelling in the wilds, characters may wish to live off the land rather than eat through rapidly diminishing (or entirely forgotten) supplies. Once per day, make an Outdoor Survival Test, modified by the harshness of conditions — for instance, Challenging (+0 SL) while travelling through a relatively welcoming section of the Drakwald, but Hard (-2 SL) if traversing a frigid pass through the Grey Mountains. Success rewards a Character with enough food for themselves, along with one other for each SL after the first.

Use of appropriate tools, such as fish hooks or a spear for fishing or a bow for hunting, may reduce the Difficulty by one step, at the GM's discretion. If game is plentiful and the Characters have access to an Animal Trap (page 310), the GM can allow the Set Trap Skill in place of Outdoor Survival.

#### **Camping**

Characters sleeping outdoors without preparation or supplies suffer a *Fatigued* Condition each day, along with risking the effects of Exposure (page 170). Finding a suitable location for a camp, with shelter and a source of water, requires an Outdoor Survival Test, modified by how hospitable the locale is — Challenging (+ 0 SL) along a well travelled route in fine weather, or Very Hard (-3 SL) in a sodden salt marsh in the wake of a momentous flood. Additional SL can make your camp harder for others to find, or compensate for the lack of proper equipment, like a tent or bed roll.

Life may be cheap in the Empire, but the necessities of living are anything but. Most commoners are used to making what they can for themselves and purchasing only those essentials they can't get by without. Clothes, simple tools, and damp hovels of mud and stone are often made by their owners, while skilled artisans create more complicated and hard wearing goods.

Smiths, artisans, and others who make their living by creating things for others can use the *Income* Endeavour (page 199) to reflect their typical business operations. Creating particular items, such as a sword, armour, or wagon, can be handled via the *Crafting* Endeavour (page 198). If there is enough time during an adventure, the GM can allow a character to undertake a *Crafting* Endeavour during play if they wish.

During an adventure, characters may wish to quickly create something they desperately need that they either cannot afford or find. An appropriate Trade Test, with a difficulty set by the complexity of the item being created, is usually sufficient. As most items take time and resources to create, the GM should rule on what is reasonable in a given situation. A skilled character in a well equipped workshop could reasonably create a simple blade with an Average **(+2 SL) Trade (Smith)** Test, while a **Very Hard (-3 SL) Trade (Shipwright)** Test would be required to lash together a small raft just from materials gathered at the river's edge.

Some other common situations are covered below.

#### **Repairing Weapons**

Weapons can be repaired with an **Average (+2 SL) Extended Trade (Smith)** Test, so long as the character has access to Trade Tools and, if the weapon has suffered more than a single point of damage, a Workshop. Success requires 5 SL per point of Damage, and each Test takes 1 hour.

Weapons can be fixed by appropriate artisans for 10% of the weapon's cost per Damage point sustained. Weapons reduced to Improvised Weapons cannot be repaired.

#### **Repairing Armour**

Armour can be repaired with an **Average (+2 SL) Extended Trade (Smith)** Test, so long as the character has access to Trade Tools and, for Plate Armour, a Workshop. Success requires 5 SL per point of Damage, and each Test takes 1 hour.

**CUNNING CRAFTS** V Paying to have armour repaired costs 10% of the armour's base price per AP lost to repair it. If a section of your armour is completely broken, it will cost you 30% of the armour's base cost to fix just that area, and you will likely be waiting some time before it is completed.

# **Making Medicine**

Advisable or not, characters may attempt to create their own medicines. If they are skilled enough, these cures may be far more effective than the cures and ointments available from apothecaries and travelling charlatans that plague the Empire.

There are two broad categories of medicines: herbal remedies that are common among the poorer rural folk, and the alchemical treatments sold by apothecaries which are more frequently found in towns and cities.

#### **Locating Ingredients**

You can find appropriate herbs out in the wild if you pass an **Extended Average (+2 SL) Lore (Herbs)** Test. Each Test represents an hour of searching and the required SL on the Test depends on the time of year.

In many villages and a few towns, you may buy herbal ingredients at a rate of 5 pennies for each SL that would have been required to find them yourself.

Unlike creating a herbal remedy, the ingredients for alchemical preparations are produced through painstaking processes and must be purchased rather than found. The typical costs for a given treatment are provided on the Remedy Creation Difficulty table.

# **GETTING AROUND**

You have to keep on your toes to stay ahead in the Empire, whether slipping between throngs of people baying for blood at a public execution or leaping across the crumbling rooftops of Altdorf. This section deals with moving through the space immediately around you — if you want to cover longer distances, look to **Navigation** on page 152 instead.

No Test is required for most kinds of movement. If you want something on the other side of a room, you can simply go get it. Equally, so long as you have at least one Advance in an appropriate Ride or Drive skill, handling a mount or vehicle under normal conditions does not require the use of any dice.

Moving during combat, along with exactly how much distance you can cover in a Turn, and how to keep track of where you are on a map or with miniatures, is covered in more detail on page 163.

#### **Climbing**

Ascending a well-made ladder, even while holding a one-handed weapon, does not require a Test. You move at half rate up or down ladders or other easily climbed surfaces. If you want to climb more quickly, spend your Action to make an **Average**  **(+2 SL) Climb** Test. You will Climb an extra Movement + SL yards. So, a character with Movement 4 who rolled +2 SL will climb an additional 6 yards (4+2=6).

If both hands are free, you can climb other surfaces with suitable handholds with a Climb Test using your Action for the Turn. You ascend or descend at a rate of ½ Movement + SL in yards.

Climbing difficulty is set by the GM and varies with the nature of the climbed surface. Some climbs will be beyond the ability of most characters without the *Scale Sheer Surface* Talent (see page 125).

#### **Leaping**

Jumping to reach high ledges, leaping from rooftop to rooftop, or failing both and plummeting to your death are all essential parts of the **WFRP** experience. These require an Athletics Test, though you may substitute a Perform (Acrobatics) Test if you take a moment to describe your acrobatic feats. Failure indicates you have fallen, though a Marginal Failure (-0 SL) gives someone a chance to catch you at the last moment.

Difficulty is determined by the Character's Movement, with values 3 or below increasing Difficulty, and values of 5 or greater reducing it.

### **HOW FAR, THOUGH?**

Generally speaking, you will only need to know if your Character made a particular jump or not. With a decent run-up and a successful Athletics Test, most Characters can leap around their Movement Characteristic in yards. Additional SL after the first can increase this by up to twice that distance, but very few save the most athletic individuals can manage further than that.

#### **Jumping Down**

A fall can result in quite a bit of harm (page 169), but an intentional jump down gives you time to brace for the impact. When jumping down, make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Athletics**  Test. If you succeed, reduce the effective distance of the fall by one yard, and one more for each SL after the first. If this reduces the effective distance to zero, you suffer no damage at all, landing with a graceful roll.

# **Swimming**

A single Advance in Swim is enough to tread water without making a Test — without it, you immediately begin Drowning (page 169) in deep water. You can swim at half your Movement Speed, and a Swim Test can increase the distance covered in the same way as Athletics can when Running (page 163), and generally is used in place of Athletics and Dodge when submerged in water.

Rescuing someone who is drowning requires a **Difficult (-1 SL) Swim** Test, so long as the victim passes a Cool Test. If they don't, your Swim Test is Opposed by their Strength, regardless of their wishes.

# **Riding a Mount**

When riding a Mount in most conditions, simply replace your Movement speed with your mount's. Tests are required only in difficult circumstances, such as traversing rough terrain or fording a river on a skittish horse. Most Tests should use the rider's Skills rather than the mount's. Unusual actions taken while mounted, such as leaping from the back of a horse onto a wagon or pulling an ally onto your mount at full gallop, use either the appropriate Skill for that action or the Ride Skill, whichever is lower. Failure usually results in Falling (page 169), typically for a distance of 2 yards — about the height of a horse.

For more on mounts in combat, see page 168. Mounts that do not have the *Trained (War)* Trait are worse than useless in combat. Apply Disadvantage to every Test involving them, and most likely they will simply flee regardless of the rider's wishes.

# **Vehicle Movement**

More complex rules for vehicles can be found in various supplements, but the following rules are more than sufficient for most circumstances.

#### **Drive**

Carts, wagons, steam tanks, and odd dwarfish contraptions rely upon the Drive Skill. Tests are required only to push a vehicle beyond the speeds it was designed for, to keep a damaged vehicle moving, or to engage in thrilling **Pursuits**. Vehicle speeds vary widely, but their Movement is generally the same as that of the animals that haul it. Vehicles cannot stop instantly, and most continue travelling a distance equal to their Movement in yards even after their driver has pulled upon the reins. Ensuing crashes are dangerous for beast, vehicle, and passengers, and should be treated as Falling (page 169) a distance equal to half that travelled on the vehicle's last Turn.

Vehicles can be repaired with a use of the appropriate Trade Skill (page 114), while Animal Care is used to tend to its draught animals.

#### **Row**

Fishing from the deck of a humble rowboat has saved more than one Reikland village from starvation. Rowboats are slow, reliable, and ubiquitous. They have an effective movement of 4, reduced by as much as half while heading upriver or while overladen with goods, and increased by a similar amount when travelling with a current. A **Challenging (+0 SL) Row** Test can increase the speed by 1, or 2 on an **Astounding Success (+5 SL)**, but leaves the rower *Fatigued*. Otherwise, Row Tests are only required to navigate difficult sections of a river or other obstacle.

#### **Sail**

Sailing ships ply the seas of the Old World, most engaged in trade or exploration. A few of the Empire's rivers are large enough to accommodate sailing vessels, including the mighty Reik and Talabec Rivers. A single Advance in the Sail Skill is enough to manage ordinary duties without a Test, while rough conditions, unexpected obstacles, or insufficient crew for a larger vessel require a Sail Test with a Difficulty determined by the circumstances.

The speed of sailing vessels depends largely on the wind, but in even moderate conditions they can easily outrun a simple rowboat, even if tacking into the wind, automatically winning any Pursuit.

Sail can also be used to tie various knots that can restrain others, come free with the right tug, and other inventive uses.

# **Pursuits**

In a pursuit, those trying to escape are the Quarry, while those trying to catch them are the Pursuers. At the start of the chase, the fastest Pursuer — the one with the highest Movement makes an Opposed Test against the slowest member of the Quarry — the one with the lowest Movement. This is usually an Athletics Test, though it may instead be a Drive, Ride, or Swim Test, depending on the circumstances. If mounts or vehicles are involved, use their Movement values to determine the fastest Pursuer and slowest Quarry.

If either side wins the Opposed Test by 6 or more SL, the pursuit immediately ends in their favour (see below). Likewise, if the slowest member of one side has at least double the Movement of the fastest member of the other, they automatically win the pursuit.

These rules are intended to create interesting narrative moments, and so work slightly differently depending on whether the players are Quarry or Pursuers.

If the players are chasing one another, simply resolve the pursuit with an Opposed Athletics, Drive, Ride, Swim, or other appropriate Test instead.

#### **Pursuit Circumstances**

It is far easier to escape a dogged pursuer in the midst of a crowded town market than it is across a barren plain with nowhere to hide. Use the table below to determine how easy or difficult it is for the Characters to succeed. Each item in the table is a factor in the Character's favour, giving a result between 0 and 3.

Each Character involved in a Pursuit will encounter an Obstacle. How many Characters must successfully overcome their Obstacle to succeed in the Pursuit depends on the number of Pursuit Factors in their favour.

V **No Positive Factors**: The enemy has the upper hand! Every member of the party must successfully evade their Obstacle.

- **1-2 Positive Factors**: Half the party, rounded up, must navigate their Obstacle.
- **3+ Positive Factors:** Only one party member needs to overcome their Obstacle.

#### **Obstacles**

Obstacles can be physical barriers, such as a barred door, social challenges, such as a gate guard, or anything else the GM deems appropriate. A table of example Obstacles is provided below, which the GM may customise to suit the situation, either rolling a d10 to generate one at random or choosing whichever seems most appropriate.

If a Character succeeds with an Astounding Success (6+ SL), they may use their copious free time to grant another Character Advantage on their Test.

Characters may also make use of appropriate Trappings to gain Advantage on their Tests — such as using a Rope to help with a Climb Test, for example. The GM has final say on what Trappings may be used this way.

#### **Concluding the Chase**

If you satisfy the outcome conditions imposed by your Pursuit Factors, you win, otherwise you fail.

**If you're the Quarry and you win:** You get away. That's it, good job.

**If you're the Pursuer and you win:** You catch up with your quarry, cornering and confronting them. This may lead immediately to combat, an arrest, an interrogation, or any other appropriate confrontation. Regardless, the Quarry cannot immediately escape.

**If you're the Quarry and you lose:** The Pursuers catch and corner you. This may lead to combat, arrest, or another appropriate outcome. Any members of the Quarry who overcame their Obstacles may still escape, however, abandoning their companions to their fate.

**If you're the Pursuer and you lose:** The Quarry escapes. They may leave behind clues or evidence that could help you track them down later, but for now they have given you the slip. Any Pursuers who overcame their Obstacles may press on without the others, each gaining a single opportunity to attack or restrain the slowest member of the Quarry. Bear in mind this may leave a lone Character outnumbered and far from their companions.

# **COMBAT**

Sooner or later, you will need to stop someone in their tracks — or they will try to stop you. When diplomacy fails, is never attempted, or simply isn't understood, combat begins.

Combat uses the same core rules as the rest of the game, but actions are resolved moment by moment. Timing and a host of other factors become more important, so combat follows a more structured set of rules.

# **Timing**

In combat, the exact timing of actions matters more, so time is organised into:

- **Rounds:** A Round is enough time for all Characters to attempt a Test and move into position. It is normally just a few seconds, the GM can decide exactly how long if necessary.
- **Turns:** During a Round, each combatant has a Turn to perform an Action and a Move.
- **Initiative Order:** Each combatant usually takes their Turn in order of their *Initiative* Characteristic, from highest to lowest.

# **Combat Summary**

Combat follows these steps until one side flees or is defeated:

- 1. **Determine Surprise:** The GM determines if any Characters are *Surprised*. This normally only happens in the first Round of combat.
- 2. **Round Begins:** If the rules call for something to happen at the start of the Round, it happens now.
- 3. **Characters Take Turns:** Each combatant takes a Turn in Combat Initiative order, starting with the highest *Initiative*. Each Character can normally perform a Move and an Action on their Turn (see page 162).
- 4. **Round Ends:** The Round ends when all combatants have taken a Turn. If the rules call for something to happen at the end of the Round, it happens now.
- 5. **Repeat Steps 2–5 As Required:** Continue to play through Rounds until the combat is resolved.

# **Combat Initiative Order** V

Combatants take their Turns in Combat Initiative order. This is determined at the start of any combat by rolling a d10 and adding the character's Initiative Bonus — the GM may choose to roll for several or all NPCs at once. Those with higher results act before those with lower results. If two or more combatants have the same Combat Initiative, the one with the higher Initiative acts first. If they also have the same Initiative, they make an Opposed Initiative Test, with the winner choosing who acts first. Some Talents can modify Initiative order (see **Chapter 4: Skills and Talents**).

**Example:** *Tollich and Perdita face down a masked coven of chanting cultists. Tollich, with an* Initiative *of 38, and therefore an* Initiative Bonus *of 3, rolls a d10 and gets a result of 5 — a total of 8. His companion, Perdita, has an* Initiative *of 43, so has an* Initiative Bonus *of 4. She rolls a 6 for a total of 10. Perdita (10) acts before Tollich (8) in this combat.*

*The cultists all have an Initiative of 35. The GM rolls for all of them at once, with a final result of 8. Perdita's result of 10 means she acts first, but Tollich and the cultists both have a result of 8. However, as Tollich has the higher Initiative score (38 vs 35), he acts before the cultsists.*

# **Surprise**

Catching your enemies unawares can give you a decisive advantage. Characters might achieve Surprise by hiding, approaching from an unexpected direction, exploiting a distraction, or suddenly attacking unwary opponents. Clever plans may also catch the enemy off guard — the GM has the final say on the possibility of Surprise.

If there is a chance the ambushers will be detected, make an Opposed Stealth/Perception Test, usually between the worst Stealth among the ambushers and the best Perception among their opponents. If the ambushers win, each opponent gains the *Surprised* Condition (page 187). Otherwise, combat begins normally.

# **ROUNDS OUTSIDE COMBAT**

Outside combat, actions do not usually need to be resolved in Rounds. The GM may use them whenever timing matters or to ensure everyone has a chance to act, however, such as during an Extended Test (page 131).

# **TAKING YOUR TURN**

Combat is fast, chaotic, and constantly changing. Blades flash, arrows fly, and combatants weave between friend and foe in a desperate struggle for survival.

At the start of your Turn, the GM describes the situation and answers any questions you have about your surroundings. *'Can I see a rope connecting the chandelier to the ceiling? Could an arrow cut through it?'*, for example. Once you know what is happening, declare what you want to do.

On your Turn, you normally have one Move and one Action, which you can take in either order. Narratively, these happen together, so feel free to describe them as a single manoeuvre. You can also skip taking a Move or Action, though once your Turn ends you must wait until the next Round to act again.

Vivid descriptions help bring the combat to life. Instead of saying, *'I attack the goblin,'* you might say, *'I leap from my chair, sprint towards the goblin, and lunge with the tip of my sword.'* The GM might even reward you for great roleplaying. The same goes for the resolution of your Action — the GM describes the outcome, but you can certainly embellish it!

# **Move**

In most situations, exact distances are unimportant. Use your Movement Attribute as a guide, and the GM will tell you if a destination is too far to reach in a single Turn.

Difficult movement, such as climbing or jumping (page 156), may require a Skill Test. If so, it also uses your Action.

For more on movement, charging, disengaging, and fleeing, see **Moving in Combat** below.

# **Action**

Your Action is used to do something in combat, whether that is attacking a mutant, leaping between rooftops, or pausing to assess the situation. What you can attempt is limited only by imagination, your Character's abilities, the environment, and the GM's judgement.

Describe what you want to do. The GM decides whether a Test is required. The results are then narrated by you and the GM, ending your Action.

For detailed rules on attacking, see **Attacking** (page 164). For prayers and magic, see **Chapter 7: Religion and Belief** or **Chapter 8: Magic**. **Chapter 4: Skills and Talents** also contains many examples of using Skills and Talents in combat.

**Example:** *Molli watches nervously from behind a barrel as Salundra is beset by three pub brawlers determined to put the young captain in her place. On her Turn, Molli's Player asks whether the inn has an upper floor. The GM confirms there is a balcony overlooking the fight, and the Player declares that Molli will sprint upstairs and shove a table over the railing, using both her Move and Action.*

*The balcony is close enough to reach in a single Turn, but heaving the table over the edge requires an Average (+2 SL) Athletics Test. Molli's Athletics is 34, so she needs 54 or less to succeed. She rolls 21, scoring +3 SL, and the table crashes down!*

*'Stop fighting!' Molli shouts as it falls. The GM rules that everyone below must make an Opposed Dodge Test against +3 SL or be struck by the table. Unfortunately, the only combatant to fail is Salundra and the table lands squarely on her head...*

#### **Free Actions**

Some things do not use your Action, such as shouting a warning, drawing a weapon, or drinking a potion. These are Free Actions and do not count towards your limit of a Move and an Action in a Round.

The GM decides what counts as a Free Action, but as a general rule, anything that requires a Test is an Action. There is no strict limit to the number of Free Actions you can take in a Round, but common sense applies — you may shout a warning and quickly quaff a potion, but attempting to drink half a dozen dubious concoctions in a single round is not reasonable. The GM is the final arbiter of such questions.

#### **On the Defensive**

Sometimes it is better to prepare than act. As your Action, choose a Skil**l** to use defensively. Until the start of your next Turn, you gain +2 SL on defensive Tests using that Skill.

This might mean preparing to dodge or parry incoming attacks, holding a doorway, readying Language (Magick) to counter hostile Spells, or anything else you can think of and the GM agrees to.

# **MOVING IN COMBAT** V

How much detail you need for movement depends on how you track positions. In 'theatre of the mind', relative positions are described and the GM adjudicates movement as required. Even during tightly controlled combat Rounds, it is usually enough to narrate how your character negotiates the frenetic action, leaping barrels, sprinting for cover, or dancing around opponents as required.

You may alternatively wish to use a grid or battle map, with tokens or Citadel Miniatures to track positions. We recommend a 1-inch grid, with each square representing 2 yards. A character with Movement 4 can therefore move 4 squares. Human-sized creatures occupy a single square, while larger creatures occupy more according to their Size Trait (page 360). If playing without a grid, you can measure distances, where 1 Movement equals 1 inch on the board.

The **Movement Table** shows how many yards you can normally move in a single Turn, either Walking or Running, without having to make an Athletics Test to sprint. Doing this will use your Move for your Turn.

# **ENGAGED**

When you make or are the target of a Melee Attack, you become Engaged with your opponent. This represents two combatants locked in close combat.

If neither of you attacks the other for a full Round, you are no longer Engaged.

If an Engaged opponent uses their Move and Action to flee, attempting to catch them may trigger a Pursuit (page 158).

#### **Running**

On your Turn, you can use your Action to sprint. This requires an **Average (+2 SL) Athletics** Test, and the distance covered is in addition to your Move this round. You sprint your Run movement + SL in yards (see the **Movement Table** for your Run movement). So, a character with Move 4 who rolled -2 SL would sprint an additional 14 yards (16-2=14).

#### **Charging**

If you are not already Engaged, you may use your Move to Charge. If you do, your Action must be a Melee Test. If your target was at least your Move in yards away before you Charged, but within your Run range (see the **Movement Table**), you also gain Momentum as you barrel into your opponent.

## **Disengaging**

If you are Engaged and wish to leave combat, you have two options.

If you have Momentum, you may lose it to move away from your opponents without penalty. You may then move normally, including Charging another target, Running, or retreating to attack at range.

If you do not have Momentum, you must use your Action to make a Dodge or Perform (Acrobatics) Test, opposed by the highest Melee Skill among foes you are Engaged with. While Outnumbered (page 167), make this Test with Disadvantage. If you are Surrounded (page 167), you cannot Disengage at all.

If you succeed, you are no longer Engaged and may move normally. Should you fail, your opponents gain Momentum and you remain Engaged.

# **Fleeing**

If you cannot escape otherwise, you may Flee as your Move. Fleeing is often involuntary, caused by *Terror* (page 184) or magic, or may be your only option if you cannot Disengage. You may Flee even if you are Surrounded, throwing yourself into your enemies' blades for the merest chance at escape.

When you choose to Flee, each Engaged opponent immediately gains Momentum, which they may lose to make one free Melee Attack against you. You gain 1 *Broken* Condition for each enemy you were Engaged with.

# **ATTACKING**

To make a Ranged Attack, your target must be within your weapon's range (see **Chapter 11: Consumer Guide**) and in your line of sight. To make a Melee Attack, you must be Engaged with your target (page 163) and have a weapon ready.

# **1: Roll to Hit**

**Melee:** Make an Opposed Melee Test against your target. The combatant with the higher SL wins (see page 130).

**Ranged:** Make a Ranged Test using your weapon. On a success, you hit. On a failure, your attack misses.

In both melee and ranged combat, rolling double on a success is a Critical Hit, while a failed double is a Fumble (see **Critical Hits and Fumbles** below).

**Summary:** Test to hit your opponent with your weapon. This Test is Opposed in melee.

# **2: Determine Hit Location**

If your attack hits, reverse the digits of your successful hit roll and compare the result to the **Hit Locations** table. For example, a roll of 23 becomes 32, striking the right arm.

Creatures with unusual anatomies may use different **Hit Location** tables (see **Chapter 12: Bestiary**).

| d100  | Location                    |
|-------|-----------------------------|
| 01–09 | Head                        |
| 10–24 | Left Arm (or Secondary Arm) |
| 25–44 | Right Arm (or Primary Arm)  |
| 45–79 | Body                        |
| 80–89 | Left Leg                    |
| 90–00 | Right Leg                   |

**Summary:** Reverse your 'roll to hit' Test result to determine a Hit Location.

#### **3: Determine Damage**

Each weapon has a Weapon Damage (see pages 301 and 303). This is usually your modified Strength Bonus for melee weapons, or a fixed number for ranged weapons.

The Damage you deal on a successful attack is your weapon's Weapon Damage plus the SL of your attack. For Melee Attacks, use the final SL of the Opposed Melee Test. For Ranged Attacks, use the SL of your successful Ranged Test.

**Summary:** Damage = Weapon Damage + SL

### **4: Apply Damage**

Subtract your target's Toughness Bonus and the Armour Points protecting the struck Hit Location from your Damage. The remainder is the number of Wounds they lose.

An attack that inflicts Damage always causes at least 1 Wound, even if Toughness Bonus and Armour reduce the total to 0 or less.

You cannot reduce Wounds below 0. If the Wounds lost exceed the target's remaining Wounds, they suffer a Critical Wound (see page 171).

**Summary:** Wounds Lost (minimum 1) = Damage − Toughness Bonus − Armour Points

#### **DAMAGING WEAPONS AND ARMOUR**

**Damaged Weapons** 

Some Fumble results (see page 165) or Spells may damage your weapon. For each point of Damage your weapon receives, reduce its Damage by –1. If the Damage is reduced to +0 (or +SB + 0), the weapon is mangled beyond all recognition, and now counts as an Improvised Weapon. If an Improvised Weapon is damaged, it is considered useless for melee combat.

#### **Damaged Armour**

As blows rain down on your armour, it can be damaged, possibly permanently. Whenever you are instructed to damage a piece of armour, reduce the AP in the damaged Hit Location by 1.

If this lowers the AP to 0 in that Location, the armour there is rendered useless.

# **Critical Hits and Fumbles** V

In the cut and thrust of combat, moments of extraordinary skill and disastrous mistakes can happen. These are represented by Critical Hits and Fumbles.

# **Critical Hits**

Any successful Melee or Ranged Test that rolls a double is a Critical Hit, even if you are defending in an Opposed Test.

A Critical Hit inflicts an immediate Critical Wound (see page 171). Beyond that, resolve the attack normally, including calculating SL and determining the winner of any Opposed Test.

# **Fumbles**

Any failed Melee or Ranged Test that rolls a double is a Fumble.

By default, you drop your weapon and must spend your next Action retrieving it. Alternatively, the GM may roll on the **Oops! Table**.

#### **Opposed Tests and Fumbles**

In an Opposed Test, you can Fumble and still win if your SL is higher than your opponent's. This is fine, and not a little hilarious, as you ineffectually tussle with your even worse opponent, potentially injuring yourself in the process.

**Example:** *Molli attacks with her dagger and rolls 66, a Fumble with -3 SL. Her opponent rolls 92 for -5 SL. Molli wins the Opposed Test by 2 SL, but still suffers the effects of her Fumble, rolling on the Oops! Table to see what happens.*

#### **Misfires!**

If you are using a Blackpowder, Engineering, or Explosive weapon and roll 99 or 00, your weapon Misfires: roll on the **Misfire Table** instead of the **Oops! Table**.

# **Ranged Combat**

There are some extra considerations that apply to ranged attacks:

| d10  | Result                                                                                                  |
|------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1–8  | The weapon fails to fire. If a gun, it must be reloaded. If an explosive, it is a dud.               |
| 9–10 | Catastrophe! The weapon explodes, dealing full Damage to your primary arm with +0 SL for the hit. |

- Ranged Attacks cannot normally be opposed with Melee Skills. At Point Blank Range (page 166), however, the target may oppose the attack with Dodge.
- You cannot make Ranged Attacks while Engaged, unless your weapon has the Pistol Quality (see page 305).
- If you make a Ranged Attack against a target you are Engaged with, they may oppose the attack with any Melee Skill.

#### **Weapon Groups**

Knowing how to swing a sword doesn't necessarily make you an expert with a spear, and the technique and understanding required to use a bow is quite different from the skills required to use a blackpowder weapon. When you use a weapon, roll against the appropriate Skill Specialisation, such as Melee (Basic) or Ranged (Blackpowder). Weapons are broken into various Weapon Groups, each tied to a Skill Specialisation.

If you are using a Melee Weapon from a Weapon Group you have no Advances in, you may still Test against your Weapon Skill. In such a case, you do not benefit from its Qualities (page 304), but your attack still suffers from the weapon's Flaws.

Ranged Weapons are typically more complicated, and you cannot make Ranged Attacks with a weapon from a Weapon Group you have no Advances in, save for a few exceptions:

- Ranged (Engineering) can be used with Blackpowder and Explosive weapons.
- You may also use Engineering Weapons with Ranged (Blackpowder), but such Tests are always at least Hard (-2 SL).
- You may make a Ballistic Skill Test to attack with Crossbow and Throwing weapons, but these attacks are always at least Difficult (-1 SL).

#### **Melee Weapon Group Special Rules**

Some Melee Weapon Groups have special rules.

- On foot, you may use a two handed Cavalry Weapon with Melee (Two-handed) with no penalty, if you prefer.
- Single-handed Cavalry Weapons count as Improvised Weapons when on foot.
- You may use a Fencing Weapon with Melee (Basic), but cannot use its Qualities when doing so.
- Using a Flail without any Advances in Melee (Flail) causes such weapons to gain the *Dangerous* Flaw.

#### **Melee Weapon Reach**

Weapon reach (page 300) has the following impacts:

- When opposing a Melee Attack Test, you gain +1 SL if the attacker's weapon is shorter than yours.
- If your weapon is Very Long, you can Engage enemies up to 4 yards away, or 6 yards if its Reach is Massive.

# **Combat Modifiers**

Combat Tests are usually Challenging (+0 SL). Circumstances may grant an SL modifier or penalty to Ranged or Melee Tests.

These modifiers apply to Attack Tests only, not to Tests made to oppose an attack. If an attacker gains Advantage on a Melee Test due to outnumbering their target, the defender does not automatically suffer Disadvantage on a Melee Test made to parry that blow simply because they are outnumbered.

# **Called Shots**

If you wish to target a specific Hit Location, you take a -2 SL penalty to your Attack Test. If you succeed, the attack hits whichever location you wish.

# **Helpless Targets**

When making a Melee Attack (or a Ranged Attack at Point Blank Range) against a sleeping, unconscious, or otherwise Helpless target, you choose the result of your attack roll instead of rolling. You automatically hit and may choose the Hit Location if you score a Critical Hit.

If the GM prefers a more lethal game, any such attack against a Helpless target simply kills them.

If you can Oppose an attack but choose not to, you count as Helpless.

# **Outnumbering**

You outnumber an opponent only if your side has at least twice as many Engaged combatants as theirs. Only combatants Engaged with one another count when determining this. If there is any doubt, the GM decides who outnumbers whom.

#### **Surrounded**

A target is surrounded if they have no route of escape. This usually requires at least three attackers, though two may suffice in a confined space such as a tunnel or narrow hallway.

# **Shooting into a Group**

Shooting into a group of half a dozen or more grants Advantage, but you cannot choose which member of the group you hit, including any allies mixed into the fray. On a successful attack, the GM randomly determines the target from among the likely candidates.

# **Shooting into Melee**

Ranged attacks against Engaged targets suffer Disadvantage. If this causes an attack that would otherwise have hit to miss, you instead hit one of the target's Engaged opponents, chosen at random by the GM.

If you do not care who you hit, treat the melee as a group instead (see **Shooting into a Group**).

# **Two-weapon Fighting**

Some warriors fight with a weapon in each hand, such as a sword and sword-breaker, or an axe and pistol.

When fighting with two weapons:

- You may wield any two one-handed weapons.
- After attacking with one weapon, you may spend Momentum (page 168) to immediately attack with the weapon in your other hand.
- Attacks made with your off hand suffer -2 SL.

**Note:** The *Ambidextrous* and *Dual Wielder* Talents can mitigate penalties associated with two-weapon fighting.

# **Unarmed Combat**

Not every fight in **WFRP** involves guns or swords. Many conflicts are settled with good old-fashioned fisticuffs. A successful Melee (Brawling) Test for unarmed combat is handled in the same way as any other combat Test, but you have one extra option: you may enter a Grapple!

#### **Grappling**

Instead of inflicting damage with an unarmed attack, you can attempt to Grapple and immobilise your opponent. You must declare this is your intent before rolling to hit your opponent. If you win the Opposed Test, you and your opponent are Grappling and your opponent gains an *Entangled* Condition (page 186). If you begin your Turn Grappling, you must make an Opposed Strength Test for your Action. If you win, you can do one of the following:

- Deal SB + SL Damage using your Strength Test roll to determine the Hit Location affected. You ignore any Armour Points as you wrench arms and pull muscles.
- Give your opponent an *Entangled* Condition, plus one more for each SL after the first.
- Remove an *Entangled* Condition from yourself, plus one more for each SL after the first.

# **Undamaging and Unarmed Attacks**

For those lacking claws, fangs, or some other natural enchancement, attacks made without a weapon are *Undamaging*. Any AP the target posses are doubled, attacks do not inflict a minimum of 1 Wound, and, if a Critical Wound is inflicted, roll twice on the Critical Wound table and choose the lower result.

# **Mounted Combat**

Riding into combat, sword swinging, guns blazing, is not only terrifying for those on the receiving end, it also brings bonuses to the rider. Mounted combat uses the same rules as any other combat, with the following additions:

- For the purposes of their Move, riders count as having the Movement Attribute of their mount. Further, riders take Ride Tests as Free Actions for any Tests to Sprint, Leap, Charge, or similar, and use their mount's Movement Attribute.
- Any Melee Test made with a Cavalry weapon by a rider against a target smaller than their mount gains Advantage.
- You choose whether to target a rider or their mount. If you attempt to hit the rider in melee, touch them, or similar, you suffer Disadvantage on the Test if you are smaller Size than the mount (see page 360), unless your weapon has a reach of Long or greater.
- A mount without the *Skittish* Trait (see page 361) is effectively another combatant, and may use its own Action to attack targets.
- When Charging, you may count as having your mount's Strength Bonus for the purpose of calculating Weapon Damage.
- When riding, you suffer Disadvantage on Dodge Tests unless you have the *Trick Rider* Talent.

**Note:** Most mounts are bigger than Characters, meaning they may cause *Fear* and gain other combat advantages as explained on page 360.

# **Scatter**

On a failed Ranged (Throwing) Test, roll 1d10 and consult the following diagram to see where your weapon lands. 'T' marks the Target.

| 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | T | 5 |
| 6 | 7 | 8 |

A roll of 1–8 provides a direction: roll 2d10 to determine the distance the weapon scatters in yards — it scatters no more than half the distance between you and the target. A roll of 9 and the weapon lands at your feet. A 10, and it lands at your target's feet.

Scatter can be used whenever a random direction is required.

# **MOMENTUM**

Experienced fighters know that a warrior who strikes first and strikes well is likely to win a fight. In combat, Momentum is gained by striking an opponent with a Melee Attack, and can later be used to maintain a dominant position.

All characters, including NPCs, use Momentum. It is up to each player to keep track of whether they have Momentum or not, while the GM keeps track of NPCs.

#### **Gaining Momentum**

Whenever you make a successful Opposed Melee Test against an opponent in combat, you gain Momentum. You either have Momentum or you don't, so gaining Momentum twice has no additional effect.

When you have Momentum, you gain Advantage (page 130) on any Melee Test you make. You gain this benefit even if you are attacking a character other than the one you gained Momentum 'from'. The benefits of Momentum do not apply to any other Tests.

#### **Losing Momentum**

You lose Momentum when you fail a Melee Test — including losing an Opposed Test where you gained some SL, but not enough to beat your opponent — suffer Damage, or have a Condition inflicted upon you. You also lose any Momentum when combat ends, or there is otherwise a pause in fighting. Some Talents and Creature Traits also allow you to spend Momentum to gain certain benefits, such as making an attack with a secondary weapon or vomiting acidic bile upon one's foes.

# **MOMENTUM AND EXTRA ATTACKS**

No matter the circumstances, a character or NPC may only spend Momentum to make one additional attack each Turn, even if they gain Momentum again after doing so.

A **Leadership** Test allows you to issue orders to NPCs clearly and with authority. If you're attempting to order your natural subordinates — a noble commanding serfs, or a sergeant commanding their troops — commands are usually unopposed. If there is no natural hierarchy in place, or the order is particularly challenging — such as ordering your soldiers to charge a hydra head on — the Test is Opposed by your targets' Cool. If you are contesting another's authority, make an Opposed Leadership Test — the orders of the winner will be followed, though such disagreements are usually terrible for morale.

You may use Leadership to encourage an ally, as long as you don't have the *Broken* Condition. As a Free Action, make a Leadership Test to remove a *Broken* Condition from one ally of your choice, plus one additional *Broken* Condition from that ally or another for each SL after the first.

Leadership can also be used to transfer Momentum. As a Free Action, lose Momentum to make a Leadership Test. If you succeed, you may grant Momentum to one ally of your choice.

# **Intimidate in Combat**

You can threaten a foe in the hopes of leaving them demoralised. As an Action make an Intimidate Test opposed by the target's Cool. If successful, the target *Fears* you, treating you as having a *Fear* rating of 1, plus 1 more for each SL after the first.

Intimidating a group of foes raises the Difficulty of the Test to Hard (-2 SL). When fighting a well-led enemy, the Test is Opposed by the Leadership Skill of whoever is in charge.

V **Leadership in Combat** When fighting a disorganised rabble, the Test is Opposed by the lowest Cool among the foe, as fear easily spreads among such groups.

# **Non-Combat Damage**

The world is a dangerous place, hiding countless ways to die. The following covers some of the other common ways you can receive damage during play.

# **Falling**

When you fall, you suffer 3 Damage for each yard, reduced by your Toughness Bonus, but not any Armour you may be wearing. If you lose more Wounds than your Toughness Bonus, make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test. If you fail, you fall *Prone* and suffer an immediate Critical Wound to your Leg. If the result is an Astounding Failure, take an additional Critical Wound to a random location as well. If you are intentionally jumping down rather than falling, you may mitigate the Damage (see **Jumping Down** page 157).

#### **Drowning and Suffocation**

If prepared, you can hold your breath for Toughness Bonus x 10 seconds without a Test. After that, you may start to suffocate or drown. If you are unprepared and suddenly deprived of air, you will start to suffocate immediately.

Each Round you are being suffocated, you lose 1 Wound. Should you reach 0 Wounds, you immediately receive the *Unconscious* Condition. After that, you will die of suffocation or drowning in Toughness Bonus Rounds.

#### **Exposure**

Every 4 hours spent in a difficult environment — such as subzero temperatures, a hot desert, or a howling storm requires you to take an Endurance Test. Extreme environments require a Test every 2 hours.

**Cold:** Your first failure inflicts Disadvantage on Tests based on Ballistic Skill, Agility, and Dexterity. The second failure inflicts Disadvantage on all Tests. Subsequent failures cause 1d10 Damage, ignoring Armour Points. If you reach 0 Wounds, you do not suffer a Critical Wound, but immediately take the *Unconscious* Condition. You will die in Toughness Bonus hours unless you are somehow warmed up. Certain Trappings may provide bonuses or penalties to these Tests, for example a coat (page 309).

**Heat:** Your first failure inflicts Disadvantage on Tests based on Intelligence and Willpower, and adds a *Fatigued* Condition. The second failure inflicts Disadvantage on all Tests, and adds another *Fatigued* Condition. Subsequent failures cause 1d10 Damage ignoring Armour Points. If you reach 0 Wounds, you do not suffer a Critical Wound, but immediately take the *Unconscious* Condition. You will die in Toughness Bonus hours unless you are somehow cooled off. Stripping off all armour grants +1 SL on Endurance Tests to resist exposure to heat.

#### **Thirst and Starvation**

Running out of provisions can be bad. Running out of water is worse. Endurance Tests to withstand thirst and hunger become increasingly difficult (cumulative -1 SL for each Test after the first). Characters without food and water cannot heal Wounds or recover *Fatigued* naturally.

**Water:** Every day without water requires you to take an Endurance Test. The first failed Test inflicts Disadvantage on Tests based on Intelligence, Willpower, and Fellowship. Second and subsequent failures inflict Disadvantage on all Tests, and cause 1d10 Damage ignoring Armour Points. If you reach 0 Wounds, you do not suffer a Critical Wound, but reduce your Movement to 1, and you will die in Toughness Bonus days, unless you get something to drink.

**Food:** Every 2 days without food requires an Endurance Test. The first failed Test inflicts Disadvantage on Tests based on Strength and Toughness. Second and subsequent failures inflict Disadvantage on all Tests, and cause 1d10 Damage ignoring Armour Points. If you reach 0 Wounds, you do not suffer a Critical Wound, but permanently reduce your Toughness by 1d10. See **Characteristic Loss**, page 177.

# **INJURY, HEALING, AND DEATH**

Getting hurt is all but unavoidable. Combat, accidents, spells gone awry, and simple bad luck can all lead to lost Wounds, hideous infections, or an untimely trip to the Garden of Morr.

# **Wounds**

Wound loss represents minor cuts, bruises, and depleted reserves of energy. Wounds are lost by sustaining Damage (page 164). The lowest number of Wounds you can ever have is 0. If you would be taken to negative Wounds, you instead suffer a Critical Wound.

# **Healing Wounds**

At the end of each day, make an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test. If successful, you recover Wounds equal to the SL + your Toughness Bonus. If the day was spent restfully, you recover a further number of Wounds equal to your Toughness Bonus even if you failed this Test.

#### **Using the Heal Skill**

You, or another Character, can use the Heal skill to recover more Wounds, so long as they have access to bandages, poultices, or other medical supplies. A successful Heal Test recovers Intelligence Bonus + SL Wounds. The difficulty of the Test is determined by the circumstances and the nature of the injury — during Combat it is never easier than Challenging (+0 SL). A patient can only benefit from one Heal roll per incident in which they lost Wounds, such as a fight, fall, or unfortunate accident involving the graf 's horse.

A fumbled Heal Test inflicts 1d10 Wounds, but cannot reduce the target below 1 Wound.

#### **THE HEAL SKILL AND BLEEDING CONDITIONS**

Characters remove one *Bleeding* Condition for each Wound they recover. While the Heal Skill can only be used to recover Wounds once per incident, you can still use it to remove *Bleeding* Conditions — simply remove one for each Wound that would have been recovered.

When attempting to heal an animal, follow the rules for healing as laid out here but replace the Heal Skill with the Animal Care Skill.

# **OPEN WOUNDS**

If you lose Wounds and do not apply a Bandage (page 316) or have your injury seen to in some other way, such as by a successful use of the Heal Skill, you risk infection. At the end of the day, make an **Average (+ 2 SL) Endurance** Test. If you fail, you incur the *Infection* Symptom (page 182) until you are treated with a successful **Difficult (-1 SL) Heal** Test, or obtain a herbal Remedy (page 314) or other suitable cure.

# **Magic, Alchemy, and Healing**

Spells, Blessings, and some rare concoctions (page 314) can also heal Wounds, though such means are all either rare, expensive, or both.

# **Critical Wounds**

Critical Wounds are severe, and include broken bones, torn muscles, internal injuries, and sundered flesh. Critical Wounds are debilitating and dangerous. Gain too many and you risk death.

There are two ways to receive a Critical Wound. One is to suffer a Critical Hit, the other is to incur any Damage that would reduce your Wounds below 0.

#### **Inflicting Critical Wounds on an Opponent with 0 Wounds**

If Damage would reduce a character to below 0 Wounds, instead reduce them to 0 Wounds and inflict a Critical Wound. Roll a d100 on the relevant **Critical Wounds** table for the struck Location (pages 173–176). When rolling on the **Critical Wounds** table, add +10 to the result for every Wound inflicted beyond what was needed to reduce them to 0 Wounds.

#### **Inflicting Critical Wounds with Critical Hits**

Any Critical on a Melee or Ranged Test, whether attacking or defending, is a Critical Hit and inflicts a Critical Wound.

**HEALING ANIMALS** V For a Critical Wound caused by a Critical Hit, do not reverse your attack roll to find out the Location of your strike as you would do for a normal hit. Instead, roll a d100 again to work out where your hit landed (see page 164), then roll a d100 again on the relevant **Critical Wounds** table for that Location.

> Attack Tests that both result in a Critical Hit and which inflict Damage that would reduce Wounds below zero inflict two Critical Wounds — one inflicted to a hit location determined by reversing the roll, and an additional one inflicted to a random hit location, as explained above.

# **PULLING YOUR BLOWS**

The rules assume you wish to inflict every possible Critical Wound you can. It is also possible you may just want to subdue, not kill, your opponents — or you may be sparring. With that in mind, you may ignore any Critical Wound you should inflict upon your opponents if you declare you are 'pulling your blow' before you roll to hit. If you do this, make sure you describe the situation appropriately, making it clear you are using the flat of your blade or otherwise doing what you can to avoid properly hurting your opponent.

You may not pull blows with missiles, Spells, Miracles, or weapons that cause *Ablaze* Conditions. The following Weapon Qualities are lost when pulling your blows: *Damaging*, *Hack*, *Impact*, *Impale*, and *Penetrating*.

If you are trying to knock your opponent out you will need to:

- 1. Declare your intention and that you are pulling your blows.
- 2. Hit them on the Head Location.
- 3. Use a weapon with the *Pummel* Quality.
- 4. Inflict more *Stunned* Conditions than your target's Toughness Bonus.

Or use the Silent Takedown rules (page 135).

#### **Additional Wound Loss from Critical Wounds**

Use the Wounds column on the **Critical Wounds** table to determine additional Wounds lost as a result of that Critical Wound. Work out this extra damage after calculating all other effects of normal damage and Critical Wounds. Wounds caused in this way do not cause further rolls on the **Critical Wounds** table.

# **Healing Critical Wounds**

If you have received any Critical Wounds, you are said to be Critically Wounded. Critical Wounds can be very serious. Refer to the appropriate **Critical Wounds** table to see exactly what penalties and Injuries (page 176) you suffer for any Critical Wounds sustained. Critical Wounds do not count as healed until all Conditions they inflict are removed and all non-permanent negative modifiers are resolved. When you are Critically Wounded, you may still heal your Wounds using the normal rules.

# **Medical Attention**

Several Critical Wound results and conditions cannot be resolved until you receive some kind of Medical Attention. All of the following count as Medical Attention, as could other means and effects, at the GM's discretion.

- Successful use of the *Heal* Skill.
- Application of a Bandage, Healing Poultice, or similar.
- Successful use of a Prayer or Spell that heals Wounds.

#### **Surgery**

Some wounds are beyond simple stitching and foul-smelling poultices. If you suffer a Critical Wound where surgery is required to attend to it (marked 'Surgery'), you suffer the marked penalties until you visit an appropriately trained doktor or barber-surgeon, or somehow source a magical or divine surgery equivalent. A character with the Surgery Talent can treat such a Critical Wound by making a **Challenging (+0 SL) Heal** Test.

# **Death**

Death can result directly from a Critical Wound or if a Character succumbs to many injuries. If you have more Critical Wounds than your Toughness Bonus, you are at risk of death. In such circumstances, should you incur the *Unconscious* Condition from an attack, Spell, *Bleeding* Condition, or the effects of a Critical Wound, you begin to succumb to your injuries. You will perish after Toughness Bonus rounds unless you are healed of one or more Critical Wounds or the *Unconscious* Condition is removed.

#### **Sudden Death**

As the GM, you may not wish to track the details of injuries for all participants, and can instead simply determine if someone is dead or not using the Sudden Death rules. In this case, when a target reaches 0 Wounds, the GM can simply declare them dead or unconscious.

Sudden Death is useful for determining the fate of background NPCs such as brigands or low-tier cultists, but it should not be used to determine the fate of Player Characters, or for NPCs with the *Luck* Talent.

# **Injuries**

Injuries are lasting ailments inflicted by Critical Wounds that require specialist treatment or some other step to address. While you have an Injury, the Critical Wound that caused it does not count as healed.

## **Broken Bones**

Small fractures may heal on their own accord, but severe breaks, with bone protruding at odd angles from the flesh, are a different matter entirely. If you suffer a Broken Bone, it will be marked as *Minor* or *Major*.

# **CHARACTERISTIC LOSS**

Sometimes, terrible injuries, horrific spells, or other events may cause your Character to suffer a loss to their Characteristics. When this occurs, reduce the base Characteristic first. If your Character finds themselves in the unfortunate position of having a base Characteristic reduced to 0, then remove any Advances you may have purchased.

# **HEALING TIMES AND DOWNTIME**

The recovery times from various Injuries are given in days and assume that you are keeping track of the number of days that have passed. Each Downtime (page 190) is about a week long, or 8 days by the Imperial calendar (page 289).

#### **Minor**

You have fractured one of your bones, but it is still aligned, meaning it may heal of its own accord without Medical Attention. The Hit Location cannot be used until the breakage heals. For arm and leg hits, you are down to one limb as the other is too painful to use, and may require binding. Use the rules for an amputated arm or leg to represent this (see **Amputated Parts**, page 177).

For head hits, you will be down to a liquid diet and will suffer a penalty of -3 SL to all Language Tests. For body hits, your strength and mobility will be severely limited: you suffer Disadvantage on Tests based on Strength and Agility, and halve your Movement.

**Healing:** A fractured bone will take 30 + 1d10 days to heal. At the end of this, a successful **Average (+2 SL) Endurance**  Test means the bone has set well, and no long-term effects will be suffered. If the Test is failed, you suffer -1 SL to all Agility Tests when using a damaged arm, a permanent loss of -1 Movement and -10 to your Agility Characteristic for a badly healed body or leg hit, or -1 SL to spoken Language Tests for a badly healed head hit.

A successful **Average (+2 SL) Heal** Test within a week of receiving the fracture will negate the need for the Endurance Test, but the affected area must remain bound and held in place for the duration of the healing. If the binding should be undone, another **Average (+2 SL) Heal** Test enacted within a day will need to be passed to avoid the Endurance Test.

#### **Major**

One of your bones is badly broken, and is either resting at an odd angle, or has splintered at the point of breakage. It is unlikely to heal properly without Medical Attention. The Hit Location is effectively unusable until the breakage heals. Use all the same rules as for *Broken Bone (Minor)*.

**Healing:** Healing takes 10 days longer. All associated Tests are Challenging (+0 SL). If the Test is failed, you suffer –2 SL to all Agility Tests when using a damaged arm, a permanent loss of -2 Movement and -20 to your Agility Characteristic for a badly healed body or leg hit, or -2 SL to spoken Language Tests for a badly healed head hit.

#### **Torn Muscles**

Sprains or tears to your muscles or ligaments result in significant pain and possible loss of use depending upon the severity, which will be marked as *Minor* or *Major*.

#### **Minor**

One of your muscles is sprained or torn, resulting in impaired capabilities and much pain. Suffer a penalty of -1 SL to all Tests involving the Location. If a leg is hit, also halve your Movement.

**Healing:** The muscle will partially heal in 30 - Toughness Bonus days. Successful use of the Heal Skill reduces the time by 1 day, plus a further day per SL after the first — you can gain this benefit once.

#### **Major**

One of your muscles or some important tendons have been severely damaged, resulting in extreme pain and a significant loss in capability of the afflicted limb. Suffer the same effects as *Torn Muscle (Minor)*, but the penalties are -2 SL to all Tests.

**Healing:** The muscle will partially heal in 30 - Toughness Bonus days, after which the penalty for using the limb reduces to -1 SL to all relevant Tests. The muscle will completely heal after another 30 - Toughness Bonus days. Use of the Heal Skill does little but inform you of the extent of the damage.

# **Amputated Parts**

Many of the more severe Critical Wounds result in the loss of a body part, such as fingers, hands, feet, or even a whole leg! Should a Character suffer more than one severed body part, it is probably time to start looking for an appropriate replacement to mitigate any possible penalties (see **Chapter 11: Consumer Guide**).

Whenever you suffer a Critical Wound marked '**Amputation (Difficulty)**', you must pass an Endurance Test (the Difficulty is marked in the parentheses) or gain the *Prone* Condition. On a Failure (-1 SL) or worse, you also gain a *Stunned* Condition; on an Impressive Failure (-4 SL or worse), gain the *Unconscious*  Condition. All amputations require Surgery to heal properly, the associated Critical Wound does not count as healed until you visit a surgeon. See **Surgery** on page 172.

# **IMPRESSIVE SCARS**

Scars and amputations are common in the Empire, and even sought out as a mark of an accomplished duelist or street tough. Should you accumulate a sufficient number of visible scars, severed appendages, and other marks of hard-lived life, and the GM agrees, you gain a permanent bonus of +1 SL to Intimidate Tests, and incur -1 SL penalty to Charm Tests.

# **Arm**

Use the rules for lost hand, but you cannot strap a shield to the arm since you do not have one.

#### **Ear**

Losing an ear is painful, but does not lead to any measurable penalty. Should you have the bad luck to lose both your ears, you suffer Disadvantage on all hearing-based Perception Tests.

# **Eye**

Losing an eye is horrendous, but you soon learn to compensate. Should you have the misfortune to lose both eyes, you suffer Disadvantage on all Tests influenced by sight and opponents gain Advantage on Tests made to hit you in melee.

# **Fingers**

Losing a finger results in lessened grip, you suffer a -1 SL penalty to all Tests using the hand in question per finger lost. Once 4 or more fingers are lost on a hand, use the rules for a severed hand.

#### **Foot**

Losing a foot reduces your Movement by half, rounding up, permanently. Further, you lose 10 points each of Weapon Skill and Agility, and suffer Disadvantage on all Dodge and Athletics Tests. If you lose a second foot, reduce your Movement to 1, and lose another 5 points of Weapon Skill and Agility. Reduce the Characteristic penalty caused by a lost foot by the number of toes previously severed from that appendage, if any.

#### **Hand**

You suffer Disadvantage on all Tests that typically rely on the use of two hands and you cannot wield two-handed weapons; however, a shield can be strapped to the injured arm. Should the lost hand be your primary hand, remember that there is a -2 SL penalty to Melee Tests made with weapons using your off-hand. For every 100 XP you spend, you can reduce this penalty by 1 SL as you relearn how to do everything with the other hand. If you lose both hands, you will need to use prosthetics in order to manipulate objects.

#### **Leg**

Treat this as a lost foot, but you cannot use the Dodge Skill at all.

#### **Nose**

A loss of a nose is an unsettling injury. This always counts as an Impressive Scar, but the bonus and penalty increase to +2 SL and -2 SL respectively. All Tests involving the sense of smell suffer Disadvantage.

# **Teeth**

Loss of teeth may be unsightly and can make eating difficult. For every two teeth you lose, you also suffer a permanent loss of 1 from your Fellowship Characteristic.

V Further, once more than half your teeth (Humans: 16; Elves: 18; Halflings and Dwarfs: 20) are lost, you find consuming solids significantly more difficult than your peers, doubling eating times, and making some foodstuffs nigh-on impossible to eat, which is largely a matter of roleplaying and narrative description of your actions.

#### **Toes**

Loss of toes has an adverse effect on your balance. For each toe lost, suffer a permanent loss of -1 to your Weapon Skill and Agility Characteristics.

## **Tongue**

A loss of a tongue leaves you grunting and humming, or relying on sign language, for communication. You automatically fail all Language Tests involving speaking.

# **DISEASE AND INFECTION**

Plague and pestilence — the purview of Nurgle, the Chaos God of disease and despair — are an everyday fact of Old World life. Outbreaks of plague affect all levels of society, although the common folk usually fare the worst. To ward against these terrible infections, most turn to the welcoming arms of Nurgle's sworn enemy, Shallya, the goddess of mercy and healing. Those actually suffering a disease will often turn to any possible cure, ensuring wise women, herbalists, and travelling doktors are never out of business, even when most peddle quackery rather than true remedies.

A disease can be diagnosed with the Heal Skill. The Difficulty of this Test is determined by how rare the disease is, and if the character has encountered it before.

# **Disease Format**

- **Name:** The name of the disease
- A description of the disease
- **Contraction:** How the disease is contracted
- **Incubation:** How long before the victim demonstrates any Symptoms
- **Duration:** How long the Symptoms of the disease last if untreated. At the end of the duration, the disease will come to an end.
- **Symptoms:** The effects of the disease on the victim. See **Symptoms** (page 181) for a list of all the Symptoms and how they affect a Character.
- **Permanent:** Some diseases are so horrendous they carry permanent consequences. This section is only included if the affliction has such long-term effects.

# **The Litany of Pestilence**

The following offers a tiny sample of the infections, poxes, and plagues festering in dark corners of the world and is presented as inspiration for creating your own diseases.

# **Black Plague**

Historians claim that in 1111 IC, rats swarmed across the Empire and the Black Plague followed, wiping out nine in every ten souls. Unexplained outbreaks of the horrendous disease still occur today, and when they do, they invariably bring no-nonsense white nuns. The Cult of Shallya has sworn to do everything in its power to eradicate the foul disease. Ancient laws and rights granted to the Cult allow its members to mark infected areas with white-rope plague cordons, preventing anyone from entering or leaving until the outbreak is contained and all bodies correctly disposed of.

Those who do survive the Black Plague rarely ever contract it again — a small mercy for having endured such a horrendous affliction.

**Contraction:** Enact an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test for each hour, or part thereof, spent in an area infested with infected fleas, or when exposed to infected fluids. Those who have already survived a bout of the Black Plague Test only once each day, and then with Advantage.

**Incubation:** 1d10 hours **Duration:** 3d10 days

**Symptoms:** Blight, Buboes, Fever

#### **Blood Rot**

Your very blood is diseased, and your heart now pumps sickness through your body. Leeching the rot is the accepted cure, but some doktors instead cut careful incisions near the neck to free the contaminated blood, demanding the patient ingest vast quantities of healthy blood to replace what is lost. Whether the patient accepts such remedies or not, without treatment, Blood Rot is deadly, and often ends in a visit from the Mourners Guild and the Cult of Morr.

**Contraction:** As a development of another disease or a

Critical Wound. **Incubation:** Instant **Duration:** 1d10 days

**Symptoms:** Blight, Fever, Malaise

#### **Bloody Flux**

The Bloody Flux is a persistent problem in the Empire, and widely viewed to be a curse upon the impious by the gods. The foul disease causes its unfortunate victim to evacuate the bowels forcibly and frequently. The Bloody Flux is endemic in the State Armies, where it is commonly claimed to kill more soldiers than any enemy. Typical cures include eating blood pudding to replace lost humours, 'corking', and rubbing fats into the afflicted area to lessen the sting.

**Contraction:** If you fail an **Easy (+4 SL) Endurance** Test after an infected source enters the mouth.

**Incubation:** 1d10 hours **Duration:** 1d10 days

**Symptoms:** Fever, Flux, Malaise

# **Festering Wound**

Infected cuts and abrasions are commonplace, meaning many superstitions exist explaining how best to treat them. Poultices fashioned from wrapped leaves and dung, the skin of toads, and dove feathers are common. Most physicians rubbish such talk, and prefer more scientific curatives, such as mixing the gall of a black ox with three spoonfuls of the patient's urine and half a spoon of sea salt, which is then rubbed into the festering, pus-filled wound. The screams this elicits prove just how effective the remedy is.

**Contraction:** If you fail an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test after a combat with a creature with the Infected trait (see **Chapter 12: Bestiary**). You can also develop a Festering Wound from an *Infection* (page 182).

**Incubation:** 1d10 days, or instantly if developed from another

disease or *Infection*. **Duration:** 1d10 days **Symptoms:** Fever, Malaise

# **Galloping Trots**

It is said halfling food will not give you Galloping Trots, a messy affliction all too common in the Empire given just how little care is given to the proper preparation of food. Those suffering digestive distress from the cheap halfling pies on sale across Altdorf would disagree, assuming they could escape the privy for long enough…

**Incubation:** 1d10 hours **Duration:** 1d10 days

**Symptoms:** Flux, Malaise, Nausea

Annual outbreaks of Itching Pox afflict most towns and cities of the Empire. So common is it, that most temples of Shallya have vials of white paste on hand to relieve the itching of the afflicted.

**Contraction:** If you fail an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test when contact is made with an infected individual.

**Symptoms:** Pox

#### **Packer's Pox**

A common infection amongst hunters, furriers, and traders, Packer's Pox is contracted from infected cattle and sheep, including their hides and wool, and from the bodies of those killed by the disease. It starts as a small, itchy rash, but soon pink, depressed blisters spread across the whole body, concentrating on the torso and arms. Not the worst of the various poxes plaguing the Empire, but it does linger for a very long time, and can occasionally turn deadly.

Tiny mites that infest stores of hay, wheat, or flour can bring about a persistent, hacking cough. A spring of mint held to the nose and mouth can calm the tickle that irritates a sufferer's throat and lungs, but there is no reliable cure for Weevil Cough.

# **Symptoms**

This section explains how the Symptoms for each malaise manifest. Use them to create your own disgusting diseases. You can also suffer a Symptom from other causes, such as a curse, poison, or untreated Wound.

# **Blight**

You are seriously ill and perhaps close to Morr's Portal as deadly poisons flood your body. Pass an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test daily (normally when you sleep). The first time you fail this Test, you gain an *Unconscious* Condition that cannot be removed until you are no longer suffering from *Blight*. The second time you fail this Test, you perish.

#### **Buboes**

V **Sea Sickness** You have huge swellings of the lymph nodes, possibly in the groin, neck, or armpits. These are enormously painful and may bleed or seep pus. They are disgusting and stink of rot — though cultists of the Lord of Pestilence consider them a sure sign of his favour, believing they hide tiny, growing daemons. Common practice is to lance these terrible expressions of taint to remove whatever nestles within, though doing so often results in *Festering Wounds*.

> Suffer a -1 SL penalty to all physical Tests, and to all Fellowship Tests if the buboes can be seen or smelled. A Heal Test made by a character with the *Surgery* Talent can lance your buboes, removing the penalty. If the Test is failed, gain a *Festering Wound*. If your Buboes are lanced, make a **Difficult (-1 SL) Endurance** Test once per day or more swell into place.

#### **Convulsions**

Your body periodically spasms or shakes as the infection seemingly uses you like a puppet. Suffer a penalty of -1 SL to all physical Tests as your body convulses beyond your control.

# **Coughs and Sneezes**

You intermittently cough or sneeze, spreading your disease to those around you. Up to 1d10 times per session (rolled secretly at the start of the session) the GM may inform you that you feel the urge to cough and sneeze. Either make an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test or spend your next Action violently expectorating. Any creatures within 3 yards are exposed to the disease and must Test for Contraction.

#### **Fever**

Your temperature is high, you sweat and shiver, and find it difficult to keep your cool. Any Agility, Dexterity, or Psychology Tests suffer a penalty of -2 SL.

#### **Flux**

There's a rumble and a grumble. Any given situation may be the moment you just have to go to the privy. You are expected to take every opportunity you can to pop off for an essential stop, and this is largely left in your hands. In addition, once per session the GM will let you know that you simply have to go immediately. You have a number of Rounds equal to your Toughness Bonus to get to an appropriate place to relieve yourself, or else suffer the *Besmirched* Condition.

## **Infection**

You have a wound or open sore that will not heal properly because of an infection, weeping foul-smelling pus. For each *Infection* Symptom you have, you cannot recover 1 Wound. Every day, make a single **Very Easy (+6 SL) Endurance** Test or gain a *Festering Wound* if you do not already have one. If you do have one, the Test is **Easy (+4 SL)**, and if you fail, you contract *Blood Rot*.

An *Infection* Symptom caused by lost Wounds is removed once it has caused a *Festering Wound*, or once you have received treatment for the infection. An *Infection* Symptom caused by a disease remains until that disease is cured.

#### **Gangrene**

Some part of your flesh is withered and dying, infected with something awful or entirely bereft of blood flow. Roll percentile dice to determine a Hit Location (see page 164). If you roll Head, your nose is affected. If you roll Arm, it is a finger. If you roll Leg, it is a toe. If you roll Body, you suffer a *Festering Wound*.

If a finger, toe, or your nose is affected, roll an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test each day. If you pass, you hold off the Gangrene; if you fail, it grows worse. If you fail more than Toughness Bonus times, you completely lose all capability with the affected location. If this occurs, use the same rules as Amputation (see pages 177–179).

For as long as you have Gangrene, suffer a -1 SL penalty to all Fellowship Tests.

#### **Malaise**

You do not feel at all well. You speak with a nasal tone due to your thick tongue and blocked sinuses, your eyes are rheumy, your nose stuffy, and your ears clogged. You lose the ability to make use of any *Acute Sense* Talents you possess, and any Language or Perception Tests you make suffer from -2 SL.

#### **Nausea**

You feel very sick and are prone to vomiting if you move around too quickly. Whenever you spend your Turn involved in strenuous physical activity (such as running, climbing, or fighting), make an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test. If you fail, you gain 1 *Stunned* Condition and may gain the *Besmirched* Condition due to being sick or instead begin dry heaving if your stomach is empty.

#### **Pox**

You are covered in pustules, inflamed swellings, disgusting rashes, or itchy spots, which are unlikely to be your most attractive feature. Poxes are largely a cosmetic issue, resulting in a penalty of -1 SL to Fellowship Tests. Additionally, up to two times per session, the GM may announce that you are suffering from an intense desire to scratch yourself. Make an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test to resist the urge. If you fail, receive a *Fatigued* Condition that is only removed if you spend your next 1d10 Actions scratching yourself.

When the Pox ends, attempt an **Average (+2 SL) Endurance** Test. If this fails, reverse the Test dice and apply permanent scarring to that Hit Location, showing an area where you scratched and the pox there healed badly. If the Hit Location is the head, permanently lose -1 Fellowship. See page 164 to determine Hit Locations.

# **Treatment of Disease**

A character with at least one Advance in the Heal Skill may administer treatment to someone who has a disease. Each day the patient spends resting under their care, the duration of the disease is reduced by 1 additional day. A successful **Challenging (+0 SL) Heal** Test ensures that the carer does not contract the disease for that day, with each SL after the first extending this protection to one other character.

The GM may apply modifiers to this Heal Test to reflect the virulence of the disease, the suitability of conditions and materials, or the stress of your circumstances.

#### **Cures and Tonics**

A plethora of cures for all manner of ailments are available for sale, though their efficacy varies wildly. See **Chapter 11: Consumer Guide** for how to purchase medicines. Off-the-shelf cures are often unreliable and an Endurance Test is usually required to see if the medicine has an effect; the Difficulty depends on the nature of the cure and the Symptom, as shown in the table below. A success staves off the Symptom for 1 day. On an Astounding Failure (-6 SL), contract *Galloping Trots*.

Poisons can inflict some of the same symptoms as a disease, but the effects usually don't last quite as long, and treating them requires identifying the poison and administering an appropriate antidote. A **Difficult (-1 SL) Heal** Test, or an **Average (+2 SL) Trade (Poisoner)** Test correctly identifies the poison and the antidote required, which could be anything from a herbal preparation to an alchemical concoction to something far more esoteric.

Poisoning is almost always handled by having the affected Character make an Endurance Test to avoid or mitigate the effects of the poison. The effects of poisons vary. Most commonly they impose *Poisoned* Conditions on the affected Character, but they may cause sleepiness, delirium, disease Symptoms, or a combination of such factors.

#### **Dosage**

Most poisons are prepared with the intention of harming a human-sized target. A dose of poison is defined as the amount needed to force a single Endurance Test on characters of *Small* or *Average* Size. For every additional dose applied, the Difficulty of the Endurance Test required to resist the poison worsens by 1 step. When used against creatures of different Sizes, more or less poison may be required.

#### **POISONS** V **PSYCHOLOGY**

Our emotions and instincts have a powerful impact on how we react in certain circumstances. These rules bring another level of drama to your game and help bring the Old World's inhabitants to life.

# **Psychology Test**

If you are exposed to one of the following Psychology Traits, you may resist its effects by passing a Psychology Test using the Cool Skill, with the Difficulty set by the GM. A successful Test lasts until the end of the current encounter, although additional Tests may be required if circumstances change.

**Example:** *Brokk has* Animosity (Elves)*. When the dwarf encounters some haughty elves sipping daintily from their wine glasses in a tavern, he must pass a Psychology Test to control himself. He rolls a 68, which is higher than his Cool of 40. Beard bristling, he stomps over to the elves and launches into a tirade about exactly what he thinks of their so-called nimbleness!*

# **Psychological Traits**

# **Animosity (Target)**

You harbour an enmity for the Target, which will normally be a group of people or creatures, such as 'Nordlanders', 'Beastmen', or 'Nobles'. You have Disadvantage on all Fellowship based Tests while interacting with that group. Each time you encounter them, you must pass a Psychology Test or be compelled to insult, denigrate, or attack them. Once you've done so, you can begrudgingly stand their presence for a while if you must.

Becoming subject to *Fear* or *Terror* quickly outweighs even the most ardent *Animosity* — you are not compelled to insult or attack someone or something you fear or are terrified of.

# **Fear (Rating)**

*Fear* represents an extreme aversion to something. When you encounter one or more creatures causing *Fear*, make a single Extended Psychology Test unless they are known allies. Make one roll each round, with a target number of SL equal to the *Fear* Rating. When encountering multiple sources of *Fear*, this test is made against the highest *Fear* Rating among them, and passing it applies to all sources of *Fear* of equal or lower rating. Once you succeed, you are no longer subject to *Fear.*

When subject to *Fear*, you may not gain Momentum, and Melee Attacks made against you by the source of your *Fear* benefit from Advantage.

### **Frenzy**

You can work yourself into a frothing rage by howling like a wolf, flaying strips of sinful skin from your unworthy flesh, or simply embracing the deep well of bloodlust within. On your Turn, you may enter a state of *Frenzy* by passing a Willpower Test.

While subject to *Frenzy,* you are immune to all other Psychology, may make an extra Melee Attack as a Free Action each round, and inflict +1 Damage on all Melee Attacks.

While in this murderous state, you must move towards your nearest foe and attack them. You may only use your Move or Action to further this goal. You remain in *Frenzy* until all nearby foes are bested, or you become *Stunned* or fall *Unconscious.* After your *Frenzy* is over, you immediately receive a *Fatigued*  Condition.

### **Hatred (Target)**

You bear a burning hatred towards the Target, usually a group of people or creatures, such as 'Hochlanders', 'Bog Octopuses', or 'Goblins'. You can never socially interact with that group, save perhaps to bellow a blood oath of vengeance as you ready your axe for the slaughter. Each time you encounter them, you must pass a Psychology Test or be compelled to destroy the hated group by the fastest and most deadly means possible. Once you have slain your foe or driven them off, you calm down sufficiently to consider other courses of action. If you wish to restrain yourself prior to this, you may attempt another Psychology Test at the end of each round after the first.

You always gain a bonus of +1 SL on Melee and Ranged Tests against the specified group, and are immune to *Intimidate* and *Fear* (but not *Terror*) caused by your hated foe, even if you have passed a Psychology Test to control your *Hatred*.

# **Terror (Rating)**

Some creatures are so profoundly unsettling as to provoke a dire, bone-chilling terror in their foes. When you first encounter a creature causing *Terror*, make a Psychology Test unless they are a known ally. If you pass, you suffer no further effects of any *Terror* for the rest of the encounter. If you fail, you receive a number of *Broken* Conditions equal to the creature's *Terror* Rating, plus one more for every negative SL after the first. When encountering multiple sources of *Terror*, this test is made against the highest *Terror* Rating among them.

After resolving the Psychology Test, the creature causes *Fear*, with a *Fear* Rating equal to their *Terror* Rating.

# **CONDITIONS**

Conditions represent the effects of things that can happen to you in the course of your adventures. The Conditions you suffer from can be recorded on a scrap sheet of paper, or you can use chits or counters to represent them. Each Condition explains how long any effects usually last in its description; however, it is possible the cause of a Condition — such as a Spell or Critical Wound — may override this.

When you incur a Condition, you immediately lose Momentum. You can still gain Momentum while suffering from one, unless the Condition states otherwise.

# **Multiple Conditions**

You can be subject to the same Condition more than once; indeed, sometimes you will receive multiples of the same Condition from a single event. If this occurs, any penalties suffered are stacked. So, if you have 3 *Bleeding* Conditions, you are losing a worrying 3 Wounds per Round; or if you have 3 *Fatigued* Conditions, you suffer -3 SL to all Tests.

You can also be subject to multiple, different Conditions at once. When this occurs, the effects do not stack; you suffer the highest of the two penalties and apply it. So, if you had 1 *Fatigued* and the *Prone* Condition, you would suffer a -2 SL penalty to all active Tests, not -3 SL.

#### **CONDITIONS AND FORTUNE**

A Condition can be removed with a Fortune Point, so while they are debilitating, they can be managed if you are careful (see page 133).

# **Ablaze**

You are on fire! This Condition is normally applied only to flammable creatures, such as those wearing combustible clothing, though some magical and divine effects can set even the non-flammable ablaze.

At the end of each Round, you suffer 1d10 Damage, reduced by your Toughness Bonus and the Armour Points on the least protected Hit Location. Each additional *Ablaze* Condition adds +1 Damage, so three *Ablaze* Conditions inflict 1d10 + 2 Damage. If this reduces you to 0 Wounds, you suffer a Critical Wound to the Body.

A successful Athletics Test removes 1 *Ablaze* Condition, and one more for each SL after the first. The GM modifies the Test's Difficulty to reflect the circumstances — for example, smothering flames in sand is much easier than in an oil-soaked kitchen.

# **Besmirched**

You are covered in effluent, foetid blood, troll mucus, or some other foul substances. More than merely treading in rat droppings or staining your jerkin with food, to be *Besmirched* is to be saturated in noxious gunk. Until you have a chance to clean yourself, you suffer Disadvantage to all Fellowship Tests.

This Condition does not stack — you are either *Besmirched* or you are not, so you do not collect multiple *Besmirched* Conditions.

# **Bleeding**

You are bleeding badly. Lose 1 Wound at the end of every Round, ignoring Toughness Bonus and Armour Points. If you reach 0 Wounds, you no longer lose Wounds; instead, at the end of each Round, make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test to avoid falling *Unconscious*.

If you are *Unconscious*, *Bleeding*, and at 0 Wounds, then at the end of each Round roll a d10. If the result is equal to or less than the number of *Bleeding* Conditions you have, you die. If you roll a 10, remove one *Bleeding* Condition. You cannot wake up until all *Bleeding* Conditions are removed.

For each Wound you recover by any means, remove a *Bleeding*  Condition. A **Challenging (+0 SL) Heal** Test removes Intelligence Bonus + SL *Bleeding* Conditions. Applying a Bandage requires an Action, but no Test, and removes one *Bleeding* Condition — you may apply a Bandage as part of a Heal Test, even if the Test fails.

#### **Blinded**

**Master Condition List** V Because of a powerful flash of light, blood in your eyes, or a terrible wound, you cannot see, and cannot undertake any task that requires sight, such as reading or making Ranged Attacks. You suffer Disadvantage to Melee Attacks and other Tests that rely heavily on sight but do not strictly require it, such as Climb or Ride. Melee Attacks against you benefit from Advantage, unless your foe is also *Blinded.* 

> Normally, one *Blinded* Condition is removed at the end of your Turn. However, if you have received a *Blinded* Condition as a result of imbibing a poison or an injury, it may persist for a longer period.

#### **Broken**

You are terrified, defeated, panicked, or otherwise convinced you are going to die. On your Turn, you must use your Move and Action to Flee (page 163) until you are in a good hiding place beyond the sight of any enemy; then you can use your Action on a Skill that allows you to hide more effectively. You also receive a -1 SL penalty to all Tests not involving running and hiding.

You cannot Test to rally from being *Broken* if you are Engaged with an enemy (see page 163). If you are not Engaged, at the end of each Round, you may attempt a Cool Test. A success removes 1 *Broken* Condition, and one more for each SL after the first. If you spend a full Round hiding out of sight of any enemy, you lose 1 *Broken* Condition.

# **Deafened**

Whether caused by a loud noise or a blow to the head, you are temporarily unable to hear. While you possess a *Deafened*  Condition you cannot understand what other characters are saying unless you can make use of the *Lip Reading* Talent. Every minute (6 Rounds) you may make a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test. If successful, remove 1 *Deafened* Condition, plus 1 for every SL past the first.

# **Entangled**

You are wrapped in something restricting your movement; it could be ropes, spider's webbing, or an opponent's firm grasp. On your Turn, you may not Move, and all your actions involving movement of any kind suffer a penalty of -1 SL (including Grappling; see page 167). For your Action, you can remove an *Entangled* Condition if you win an Opposed Strength Test against the source of the entanglement, with each SL after the first removing an extra *Entangled* Condition.

If you have an *Entangled* Condition as a result of being caught up with something that doesn't have a Strength characteristic, refer to the **Strength of Materials** Table to decide the Strength to use in an opposed test.

#### **Fatigued**

You are exhausted or stressed, and in need of rest. You suffer a -1 SL penalty to all Tests. A *Fatigued* Condition is normally removed by rest, a Prayer, or a Spell.

How much rest is required to remove a *Fatigued* Condition depends on what caused it. One inflicted by a day's hard labour, a forced march, or ingesting poison requires a night's sleep, while one caused by an exacting fight, becoming *Frenzied* or *Stunned*, or other strenuous exertion can be removed with a few minutes to catch your breath.

*Fatigued* Conditions caused by being Overburdened (page 299), disease, exposure, or other ongoing effect are removed once that circumstance or effect ends.

# **Poisoned**

You have been poisoned or injected with venom. While *Poisoned*, you suffer a -1 SL penalty to all Tests and lose 1 Wound at the end of each Round, ignoring Toughness Bonus and Armour Points.

If you reach 0 Wounds when *Poisoned*, you cannot heal any Wounds until all *Poisoned* Conditions are removed. If you fall *Unconscious* when *Poisoned*, make an Endurance Test every Toughness Bonus Rounds or die horribly.

At the end of each Round, you may attempt an **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test. A success removes 1 *Poisoned* Condition, and one more for each SL after the first. A successful Heal Test made by you or another Character removes 1 *Poisoned* Condition, and one more for each SL after the first. The Difficulty of these Tests is determined by the poison or venom. Once all *Poisoned*  Conditions are removed, gain 1 *Fatigued* Condition.

#### **Prone**

You have fallen to the ground, possibly because you have tripped, or because you have been hit by something rather large. On your Turn, your Move can only be used to stand up or crawl at half your normal Walking pace in yards. You suffer Disadvantage to all Tests involving movement of any kind, and Melee Attacks against you benefit from Advantage.

*Prone* does not stack — you are either *Prone* or you are not. You lose the *Prone* Condition when you stand up.

#### **Stunned**

You have been struck about the head or otherwise disorientated or confused; your ears are likely ringing, and little makes sense. Your Movement is halved and you suffer a -1 SL penalty to all Tests.

At the end of each Round, you may attempt a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test. If successful, remove 1 *Stunned* Condition and one more for each SL after the first. Once all *Stunned*  Conditions are removed, gain 1 *Fatigued* Condition if you do not already have one.

If you possess more *Stunned* Conditions than your Toughness Bonus, gain the *Unconscious* Condition.

You have been caught unawares and you are not at all ready for what is about to hit you. You can take no Action or Move on your Turn and cannot defend yourself in Opposed Tests. Any opponent trying to strike you in melee gains a bonus of +2 SL.

The *Surprised* Condition does not stack, so you do not collect multiple *Surprised* Conditions even should you be technically surprised multiple times in a Round.

At the end of each Round, or after the first attempt to attack you, you lose the *Surprised* Condition.

# **Unconscious**

You are knocked out, asleep, or otherwise insensible. You can do nothing on your Turn and are completely unaware of your surroundings. You are Helpless (see page 167).

The *Unconscious* Condition does not stack. When you become *Unconscious,* lose all *Stunned* Conditions.

In most cases, you wake up after a few hours of sleep, though a loud noise or other event might wake you early. If you are *Unconscious* due to a condition or circumstance, such as *Bleeding*  or certain Injuries (pages 173–176), you wake once the effect that caused you to fall unconscious ends.

If you spend Fortune to remove *Unconscious* but have not resolved the cause of the incapacitation, you fall *Unconscious*  again at the end of the Round.

# **CORRUPTION AND MUTATION**

Few threats in the Old World are more insidious than the Ruinous Powers. These malign forces hunger to corrupt your mind, warp your flesh, and claim your soul. Corruption Points represent your slow descent into Chaos. Whenever you are exposed to corruption, you may gain Corruption Points. The more you accumulate, the closer you come to damnation, until, eventually, you begin to change…

# **Gaining Corruption Points**

Corruption Points are gained through exposure to places, people, or objects tainted by Chaos, or to situations especially favoured by the Dark Gods.

You do not Test for Corruption every time you are exposed. Instead, at the end of the session, the GM determines your greatest degree of exposure and calls for a single Corruption Test.

V **Surprised** Multiple exposures, or exposure to multiple sources, may increase the Test's Difficulty, change the Characteristic Tested, or increase the Corruption gained on a failure, at the GM's discretion. The stronger the Corrupting Influence, the more SL you must score to avoid gaining Corruption Points.

> A Corruption Test is a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance or Cool** Test, as determined by the GM. Physical corruption is usually resisted with Endurance, while spiritual corruption is resisted with Cool. The stronger the Corrupting Influence, the more SL you must score to avoid gaining Corruption Points.

> **Note:** Spellcasting, or having Spells cast on you, doesn't cause Corruption unless explicitly stated. Simply using a Chaos Lore does not require you or allies to make Corruption Tests.

There are two types of exposure:

- **Exposure:** You have physical contact with the listed source for at least 1 Turn, or spend at least 1 hour in its vicinity or under its influence.
- **Prolonged Exposure:** You have physical contact with the listed source for at least 1 hour, or spend at least 6 hours in its vicinity or under its influence.

#### **Minor Corruption**

These Corrupting Influences, although relatively trivial, still endanger a weak soul. If you fail a Test to resist Minor Corruption, gain 1 Corruption Point.

- **Exposure** to a creature with the *Corruption (Minor)* Trait, refined warpstone, or a profane artefact
- **Prolonged Exposure** to Chaos worshippers, Chaos cult temples, skaven, mutant lairs, and similar

# **Moderate Corruption**

Moderate Corrupting Influences are a danger to every soul and should be avoided under all circumstances. If you fail the Test, gain 2 Corruption Points. On a Marginal Success (+0 SL), gain 1 Corruption Point. On a Success (1+ SL), you gain no Corruption Points.

- **Exposure** to a creature with the *Corruption (Moderate)* Trait or warpstone
- **Prolonged Exposure** to refined warpstone or a Chaos-tainted artefact

#### **Major Corruption**

Few mortals can withstand a Major Corrupting Influence for long. Remain in its presence, and Chaos will begin to reshape you. For each failed Major Corruption Test, gain 3 Corruption Points. If you succeed with a Marginal Success (+0 SL), gain 2 Corruption Points. On a Success (1–3 SL), you gain 1 Corruption Point. Only if you score an Impressive Success (4+ SL) do you gain no Corruption Points.

- **Exposure** to a creature with the *Corruption (Major)* Trait
- **Prolonged Exposure** to warpstone
- Making a deal with a daemon
- Consuming refined warpstone

# **Corrupting**

No one is left unscathed by exposure to the Ruinous Powers. Eventually, even the strongest fall, their mind and bodies twisting into new, unrecognisable forms.

Should you ever gain more Corruption Points than your Willpower Bonus plus your Toughness Bonus, immediately attempt a **Challenging (+0 SL) Endurance** Test. If passed, you have managed to hold off your corruption for now but will have to Test again next time you gain Corruption Points. If you fail, then either your body or mind is about to mutate.

# **Dissolution of Body and Mind**

As corruption ravages your soul, the warping breath of Chaos whispers within, either twisting your flesh into a fresh, new form, or fracturing your psyche with exquisite knowledge it can never unlearn.

First, lose Corruption Points equal to your Willpower Bonus as you mutate. Next, roll percentile dice and refer to the following table to determine whether it is your body or your mind that blossoms anew.

Next, roll on either the **Physical Corruption Table** or the **Mental Corruption Table** to see what happens.

### **Manifestation Time**

Mutations seldom manifest immediately. They may begin as an itch, a patch of flaky skin, or a nervous tic, only later developing into a wet and blinking extra eye, unexpected feathers, or a mind twisted beyond recognition. When you generate a mutation, the GM secretly rolls 3d10. The mutation manifests within that many days.

Use the descriptions in the tables to make your mutation uniquely horrifying. If it is obvious to others, you suffer significant penalties to Fellowship Tests as appropriate. More significantly, ordinary people will likely flee from your presence, the watch will be called, you will be pursued by witch hunters, and life will swiftly become very difficult.

# **BE VERY CAREFUL**

Used sparingly, Corruption Points are great fun, but new players may not appreciate how dangerous the Ruinous Powers truly are. Since mutation is effectively a death sentence if discovered, warn players when their actions risk Corruption.

While the player's Character might not be fully aware of Chaos, they should know that tainted artefacts are best left alone, cultists should be avoided at all costs, and mysteriously glowing green rocks are never to be touched. Of course, some adventures leave no choice, but that's **WFRP**.

A corrupted mind may be less obvious than a sprouting tentacle, but it can be just as devastating. Use the description you roll to create a unique mental mutation that suits your Character, developing a believable new mindset. Embrace it as an opportunity to portray your Character in a new, potentially horrifying way.

# **Corruption Limits**

A soul can withstand only so much corruption before it finally gives way. If you ever have more physical mutations than your Toughness Bonus, or more mental mutations than your Willpower Bonus, your soul is lost. Your Character has fallen to Chaos and it is time to create a new one. Your former Character becomes an NPC under the GM's control, and you may yet encounter the wretched creature they have become…
