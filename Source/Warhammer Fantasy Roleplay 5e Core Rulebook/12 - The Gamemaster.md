# • **THE GAMEMASTER** •

Bringing the world of **Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay** to life for your friends is a richly rewarding experience. If you have never run a roleplaying game before, it might seem a little intimidating, but with a few tricks of the trade, you'll find it easy and fun.

The most important rule in a running roleplaying game is to ensure that everyone at the table, you included, has fun. This chapter will set out your role as the GM, and provide you with the tools you need to do it well, while having a good time. Let's start with what exactly the GM does.

- 0 You Tell the Story
- 0 You Decide How the World Reacts
- 0 You Decide When the Rules Are Needed
- 0 You Arbitrate the Rules

# **You Tell the Story**

As a GM, you narrate the events of the story your players embark on, and portray every non-player character (NPC) they encounter. You play the role of the ruthless tyrant demanding satisfaction for a petty grievance and the pathetic urchin seeking just a penny or two for your ailing sibling. You describe the way the wind whispers through the trees, bringing the howls of distant wolves to those with ears keen enough to discern them. Every sense is yours to convey the world to your players: sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and touch. Your descriptions are the foundations upon which all that occurs in your game will be built. You do not always have to be lavish with them, but strive to have one or two memorable scenes in every game.

#### **You Decide How the World Reacts**

Roleplaying games differ from traditional storytelling in that your players get to decide what their Characters do and say. When this happens, you get to decide how the NPCs around them respond. If the Witch Hunter decides to throw their authority around with the local graf, you're the one who chooses the graf 's reaction — possibly with the help of some dice rolls (see our next point, below). When the party foils the cult's plans and sets the watch on its leader, you tell your players what happens next: how the authorities deal with the heretic, and how the rest of the cult, still in hiding, responds to the players' actions.

In this way, you keep the story moving forward, adapting to the decisions your players make along the way. You'll usually have an idea of how the story should end, but the exact details will in large part be decided by these reactions.

# **You Decide When the Rules Are Needed**

If you read **Chapter 5**, you'll know that we've provided rules for a wide variety of eventualities. But a lot of the time, you won't need to use these rules to play the game. Very often when your players decide to do something, it just happens without any need to roll dice. This is especially true for conversations, which make up a lot of roleplaying games. When the Investigator is questioning witnesses, they will often simply tell what they know, as long as the player has chosen the right people to talk to and the correct questions to ask. Your player will only need to roll a Charm Test (or Intimidate, depending on their style of questioning) if the witness has some reason to resist.

A large part of being the GM is deciding when to bring in the rules to decide the outcome of an action, and when to just let the story continue and keep the game flowing. Which brings us to our next point.

# **FAILING FORWARD**

One common mistake first-time GMs make is asking a player to roll a Test when failing that Test will stop the adventure in its tracks. If the party needs to get past that door to find the cult's lair, don't make them roll to unlock it unless you have a plan for what happens if they fail perhaps a cultist hears them fiddling with the lock and opens it to see what's happening, getting the party inside but losing the element of surprise. If the vital next clue to the mystery is hidden in a dusty library, only call for a Test to determine how quickly it is uncovered, not *if* it is.

Every time you call for a Test, have a plan for both success and failure. Failure should move the story on, but with some negative consequences.

#### **You Arbitrate the Rules**

The rules exist as a framework to decide how actions play out in a fair and transparent way. The chance for failure is what keeps a game interesting and makes success all the sweeter. What the rules shouldn't do is get in the way of having fun.

Now and then, situations will crop up in your games where the rules don't quite apply to exactly what is happening — we've given Strength characteristics for a selection of inanimate objects, but even we couldn't predict your Flagellant would wind up Entangled by the large tub of porridge they fell into. At times like this, your job is to decide the Strength of porridge on the fly, and keep the game moving forward.

Similarly, two players might disagree on how they interpret some aspect of the rules, or even with how you interpret it. In these cases, the GM serves as the final word on how the interpretation should be decided, at least for now, so the game can continue.

This doesn't mean you rule your players with an iron fist, enforcing your way or the highway. Simply that your role is to come to a decision quickly, so the game doesn't get bogged down in arguing over rules, and everyone can get back to having fun foiling the plots of the Ruinous Powers.

There is further sound advice to be found on the running of roleplaying games on Cubicle 7's website and literally tens of thousands of hours more online from sources too numerous to list; however, the rest of this chapter is devoted specifically to the running of this game before you.

# **RUNNING THE GAME**

The Warhammer world is a dark and dangerous place, where monsters lurk in the shadows and corruption is everywhere. Suffering is common and few seek to alleviate it, seeking mostly to look after their own. Kindness is so rare, that it automatically tends to make others suspicious and they wonder what price or conditions lie behind even simple acts of kindness. Many make a practice of deception, believing that the strictly honest are fools.

Most authorities rule by force, taking what they wish, and ignoring the needs of their subjects as long as any other choice presents itself. There is no presumption of innocence before the law and little mercy to be had. Justice must be bought, or forcibly taken, for it will not be granted.

This doesn't mean that games of **WFRP** should be exercises in unrelenting misery, however. Moments of kindness and even heroism do occur, and shine all the brighter for the darkness of the setting. Devotees of Shallya tend to the sick and needy as their goddess commands. Brave soldiers face down orcs and beastmen knowing that, should they perish, their sacrifice might keep their families safe. Even witch hunters, whose zeal and methods would be considered harsh by the standards of our world, view themselves as a thin line between the civilised world and dark forces that would tear it apart.

It is also worth remembering that your players' Characters are unusual by definition. They are the protagonists of the story and could be true heroes who put their lives on the line to defeat evils common folk could never stand against. Or, they might be just as corrupt and self-serving as the majority of people in the Old World. In **WFRP**, the choice is theirs.

# **Magic and Witch Hunters**

Magic in the Old World is deeply distrusted by the vast majority of people. Even licensed Wizards should be wary of using their powers in public, lest they cause a panic among the uneducated masses. Unlicensed magic users, such as Hedge Witches and Mystics, must be even more careful, as their very existence could be viewed as a crime punishable by burning at the stake.

That's not to say that your players who choose to play a magicusing Career should be punished for it. Players choose these Careers because they want to be able to use magic, so the threat of witch hunters should remain just that, a threat, so long as the Character is careful and doesn't do anything foolish like casting obvious spells in a crowded marketplace. The threat of retribution for openly using magic helps keep the world feeling distinctly Warhammer, without bogging down your sessions with a fight or flight from witch hunters every time a spell is cast.

Your magical Characters should feel special, privy to knowledge and power that most are afraid of, rather than constantly in fear of using their abilities. With a bit of care and guile, they should be able to make use of their Spells to do things mundane folk could never dream of.

# **Gods and Religion**

Religion is everywhere in the Empire and the NPCs your Characters will encounter should reference the gods often. Most people pray before a journey, leave offerings at shrines, invoke a deity when frightened, and know the festivals and holy days of their cult. Let this appear naturally in your descriptions: a hunter muttering a prayer to Taal before entering the forest, a farmer praising Rhya for a good harvest, or a soldier touching a hammer pendant before battle.

The gods themselves should remain distant. Priests, temples, miracles, and omens can all provide evidence of divine influence, but avoid having the gods appear directly or explain their will too clearly. A Character might receive an unexpected stroke of luck after praying to Ranald, dream of Morr's garden before an important death, or find that a disease improves markedly after a visit from a Shallyan priest. Whether these events are truly divine intervention or coincidence is often best left uncertain.

Different cults should also have different attitudes, rituals, and priorities. Sigmarites may preach duty and vigilance, Ulricans honour strength and courage, Shallyans offer mercy and healing, and Verenans seek justice and knowledge. Let these beliefs shape the NPCs your Characters meet and the places they visit. The gods are not interchangeable sources of Miracles, and it follows that priests should not exist simply to hand out divine magic. They should have convictions, obligations, rivalries, and opinions about how their god's teachings apply to the world around them. Opinions that may occasionally create difficult situations, both for them and the Characters.

# **Chaos and Corruption**

The Chaos Gods are real, but very few people in the Empire actually know this. Priests warn against corruption of the mind and body caused by excessive wantonness, bloodlust, curiosity, and despair, but the names Slaanesh, Khorne, Tzeentch, and Nurgle are utterly unknown to the general populace. Witch hunters burn mutants and witches to combat sinister dark forces that seek to tear the Empire down, but even they may not know the exact nature of that which they oppose — Sigmar is against it, and that's enough for them.

When NPCs discuss the existence of cults within civilisation or fell altars tended by beastmen in the wilds, they should frame them as nebulously evil as opposed to outright calling them Chaos-worshippers. Even the members of a Chaos cult might not fully understand what they worship, believing The Fly Lord to be some minor deity that gives succour to the afflicted, rather than Nurgle himself. Beastmen probably know who they worship, but good luck having a conversation with one long enough to find out.

Player Characters will most likely start out knowing nothing of Chaos and, depending on the course of their adventures, slowly uncover the terrifying truth of its existence. This in turn will expose them to Corruption (page 187), because even knowing about Chaos can be enough to start a Character on the path to ruination.

# **NPCs with Character**

More than almost any other factor, memorable NPCs will help you bring the Old World to life. WFRP has a long, rich tradition of introducing unforgettable NPCs, be they sinister villains, voluble farmers, or amusing pigeon tenders. Not every NPC has to be impossible to forget, but having a few stand out sharply will help to characterise the Old World.

When creating a notable NPC, first ask what their purpose in your game is. Are they a potential ally, a definite villain, or fun for you to play and your players to interact with? When you know their purpose, decide what their main motivation is, e.g. what do they seek? And what are they willing to do to get it? Answering these questions will suggest how they might interact with the party. While money is certainly a common answer to the first question in the Empire and beyond, think about why. Are they trying to gather funds to open a tavern? Pay off a loan shark? Need expensive medicine for a sick relative? Just how desperate are they?

Once you know what an NPC wants, and how badly they want it, you do not have to give them a lot of physical traits, just one or two distinct details. Players can easily lose track of who's who, especially if they meet a lot of NPCs, so give them a solid feature or two to hook their memories on such as a wicked scar, distinctive tattoo, or oddly rolling gait. You do not need to note average things — only mention their height, weight, or eye colour, if they are unusual.

Finally, round them out with one or two striking traits or personality quirks — especially ones you can readily characterised at the table. Do they twiddle their fingers, stroke at a beard, or scratch themselves indelicately while chatting? What does their voice sound like and how do they speak? Do they use small words, speak quickly, or pause between each sentence.

With some practice, you may well find whole adventures will bloom from Character interactions with NPCs they like, or sometimes loathe. Both work, as long as they are motivated!

# **How Do You Folks Know Each Other?**

**Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay**'s celebrated style of random Character generation can lead to parties with particularly unusual compositions, especially if your players all let the dice fall where they may. You may well end up with a group possessing all sorts of uncommon skills, but are hard pressed to tackle much more than a goblin. Or perhaps they excel at fighting, but have all the collective charm of a peckish griffon. You can help set your game going in the correct direction by assisting your players in directing their choices during Character Creation.

You should also decide as a group how this disparate group of people know each other. A party that contains both a high elf and a dwarf probably has an interesting story of how that companionship came to be formed. Encourage your players to decide how their Characters found themselves working together and nudge details of your version of the Old World about to help their conceptions of how they formed a bond.

Humans and halflings are everywhere in the Empire and could find themselves thrown together for any number of reasons determined by their Classes, Careers, or back stories. Characters with Rogue Careers may have worked together on past jobs, Warriors might have fought together, and Riverfolk could develop friendships based on working the same stretch of the Reik. Perhaps the Watchman relies on the Thief as an informant, or the Artisan and the Engineer share a workshop. It could be as simple as the Characters all hailing from the same sleepy village or crowded Altdorf neighbourhood.

Dwarfs are relatively common in the Empire, but tend to be insular within their communities. They may, however, form strong bonds with non-dwarfs over matters of honour. A human who saves a dwarf 's life or reputation may find themselves with a stalwart companion who insists on helping until they feel they have repaid their debt. Given the longer lifespans of dwarfs, the favour may even have been done by the human Character's parent or grandparent. Dwarfs also respect those of other species whom they view as particularly honourable or dedicated to their craft, and so may strike up friendships with those they share a Class or related Career with.

Elves can be encountered in the Empire, but are a rare sight and very much hold themselves apart from other species. An elf abroad in the Empire might be tasked with gathering information for their homeland and attach themselves to a band of adventurers to further this goal. Their long lives and appreciation of art might see an elf spend years accompanying an Artisan, Entertainer, or similar to witness their talent. They may even align themselves with a band of scoundrels out of a sense of adventure, or simple curiosity.

Knowing, vaguely, what sorts of adventures you intend to run will help your players make useful choices — so it is a good idea to let them in on a few details in advance, such as if their Characters will be wandering about or staying in one general area of the Old World for a time

# **Using the Rules**

Most of what you need to know about the rules can be found in **Chapter 5**, but there are a few that the GM needs to understand in a slightly more in-depth way than players.

#### **Fate and Fortune**

Fortune can turn a disastrous situation in your Characters' favour, so make spending it feel like a meaningful choice. When a Character considers spending Fortune, ask whether they are really sure they want to use it now, or whether they might need it more later. Conversely, if they have Fortune left unused, you can ask whether they want to spend it, implying that the situation may be more dangerous than it first appears. These moments should add drama to the decision without misleading the players, making the choice to spend Fortune feel like a moment of consequence rather than simply another resource.

Fate is another matter entirely. Spending Fate is a Big Deal: it is powerful enough to cheat death. When a Character spends Fate to survive, consider when they made the decision. If they spend it before Damage is rolled (**How Did That Miss?**), their escape can seem almost miraculously fortunate. They slip and the decapitating strikes whistles harmlessly overhead or an arrow rebounds off a Sigmarite medallion. If they use Fate after seeing the result, in order to avoid death (**Not Today!**), make their survival more desperate. Perhaps they are knocked unconscious and only awaken after the battle, or are swept into fast-moving water and carried a mile downstream before reaching the bank. If they spend Fate to achieve the impossible (**I Will Not Fail!**), describe how they tap into previously unknown reserves of strength to hold the portcullis up long enough for their companions to roll under, or how the grappling hook wedges improbably in a crack of the wall, allowing them to climb up just as the beastmen round the corner. Spending Fate should always feel like a dramatic moment in the adventure.

Regaining Fate should be rare enough to feel momentous, perhaps marking the culmination of a long campaign or the achievement of an objective at considerable personal cost. Fortune, by contrast, is replenished at the start of every session. This assumes a session of around four hours. For longer sessions, replenish Fortune at narratively appropriate moments, roughly every four hours. For shorter sessions, replenish it every other session to maintain a similar level of challenge.

#### **Opposed Tests**

Something should always happen as a result of an Opposed Test. Even when both Characters achieve the same SL and the Test ends in a stalemate, use the result to point towards a way forward. Perhaps neither side gains the upper hand, but one notices a weakness, discovers a clue, or realises what must be done to break the deadlock. Avoid situations where nothing happens, as a stalemate that simply stops the game is rarely interesting.

# **Momentum**

Remember that Characters can take Momentum from enemies in ways other than winning Opposed Melee Tests. Ranged attacks that inflict a Wound, for example, or anything that inflicts a Condition can also remove Momentum.

The GM needs to keep track of which NPCs and enemies have Momentum. You could mark an 'm' beside their Wounds, or place a counter next to their model to show when they have Momentum available.

# **THE GM'S TEST TOOLKIT**

There are several components of a Test, all of which are tools for the GM to use in fine-tuning the challenge presented to the Players.

#### **SL Modifiers**

These come from the Test's Difficulty and circumstances, or from a Character's Talents, equipment, and abilities. The GM determines the Difficulty, and may award up to +1 SL for a particularly clever approach. All SL Modifiers are applied after the dice are rolled.

#### **Advantage and Disadvantage**

This ability to reverse the numbers in your roll comes from Momentum (page 167), certain Talents, Spells, and Miracles, and from spending Fortune. The GM should almost never grant Advantage or inflict Disadvantage when the rules do not specifically call for it.

**Reroll:** A reroll is when you disregard the result of a dice roll, and roll it again. You must use the second result. Some Talents allow you to reroll, as does spending a point of Fortune.

**Rolling a Double:** Rolling a double means both the tens and the units die roll the same number (11, 22, 33, 44 and so on). This is most often relevant for triggering a Critical or Fumble in combat. It gives the GM the opportunity to introduce dramatic or amusing events for good or ill — feel free to get creative in describing what happens!

### **Extended Tests**

Extended Tests are used for lengthy or demanding tasks where time, resources, or repeated attempts matter, such as repairing armour or scouring the University of Altdorf 's library for mentions of exotic artefacts. If the exact time or cost isn't important, the GM can simply decide how long the task takes for someone with a relevant skill to perform.

Here's how it works:

**Set the Goal:** The GM sets the total SL required to succeed, how often Tests can be made, any deadline, and what happens if it is missed.

**Choose the Test:** The GM determines the Skill and Difficulty or other SL modifiers for each attempt. This is usually the same each time, but may change as the task progresses.

**Track Progress:** After each Test, add or subtract your SL from the running total. A success always adds at least +1 SL, a failure always subtracts at least -1 SL, a Critical adds at least +6 SL, and a Fumble subtracts at least -6 SL.

You succeed when your total reaches the required SL. You fail if your total ever drops below 0 SL, or if you run out of attempts or time. The GM decides the consequences of failure and what, if anything, was accomplished.

Characters can often work together on an Extended Test. Each makes a Test and adds their SL to the shared total.

# **EXTEND CAUTION**

Why are Extended Tests explained here rather than in the Rules chapter? Because they are a tool for the GM to use in particular circumstances. Overused, they can bog down the game and feel anticlimactic. When a significant task needs to feel exciting, a series of repeated dice rolls without tension may fail to deliver.

Extended Tests work best for short, tense situations: you have three Turns to pick this complicated lock before the guard arrives — go! They can also show how much of a larger task the Characters can complete before time runs out, such as repairing a fortress before the orc horde arrives. Avoid using them for adventure climaxes, or when a single Test can resolve the situation just as well.

# **Awarding XP**

The secret to awarding XP is to give enough for Characters to feel they are improving without making advancement seem too easy or too fast. Aim for a noticeable bump while leaving plenty of things for players to save towards.

We recommend awarding around 100 XP per session to each player, based on the group's achievements.

- 0 Surviving the session: 50 XP
- 0 Saving an important NPC's life: 10–25 XP
- 0 Finding important information: 10–25 XP
- 0 Defeating a significant antagonist: 10–25 XP
- 0 Achieving minor objectives: 10–25 XP

You can also award bonuses for significant milestones.

- 0 Achieving the adventure's main objective: 25–100 XP
- 0 Completing a series of linked adventures: 100–200 XP

Finally, consider individual bonuses for players who played their Character in keeping with their Character concept (sometimes to their detriment!), who achieved something spectacular (intentionally or not!), or who made everyone else around the table laugh uncontrollably. Try to spread this one around and avoid simply giving it to a player who dominated the session — remember the quiet players who are playing their Character well.

# **The Dangerous Business of Getting from Here to There**

Travelling in the Old World is generally considered to be fraught with peril, especially off the busiest roads favoured by merchants and pilgrims. Even in areas of the Empire that are considered to be somewhat domesticated, there are outlaws and highwaymen to consider, to say nothing of beasts and the occasional monster. Throw in particularly inclement weather, and a road largely considered safe suddenly becomes a daunting prospect.

It is up to you exactly how you wish to represent the potential dangers of travel within your game. Some GMs like to just get on with it, making travel a mere footnote description as they swiftly move on to what they consider the good stuff. Others like to linger, depicting the odd folk and interesting sights to be seen along the way. Within the Empire proper, most trips will generally be spent on a road or along a river, especially when travelling between settlements and cities. Roads are generally held to be slightly more dangerous than river travel, but cheaper, though seldom as swift — unless you are trying to travel against the current.

### **Roads & Rivers**

The Empire's roads are crude and almost never paved, but the trade routes are especially well trod, and tend to boast the most road wardens, making them the safest by far. The further you travel from the well beaten routes though, the more likely the roads will turn to ill-kept muddy paths. You would think that the finer well-maintained roads would be the tolled ones, but tolling stations can and will turn up along even the shabbiest courses.

The various coaching companies keep networks throughout the Empire's provinces, with the routes between the major cities seeing numerous coaches throughout the day. The Four Seasons is the biggest coach company in the Reikland, and is represented along every major route.

On the best roads, roadside inns can frequently be found every fifteen to twenty miles or so, which unsurprisingly, is around the distance a human traveller on foot can make each day spent walking at a brisk pace for 7 or 8 hours. Such inns are far more sporadic, or even non-existent, away from the major coaching routes, which means travellers will have to camp alongside the road at night — not a safe prospect at all.

River travel can be easy to secure, but dedicated passenger barges generally only travel between pre-determined docks at specific settlements. Getting off at an unscheduled destination will probably require some fast-talking and a modest bribe. Going almost anywhere else requires either a chartered boat, an expensive prospect, or arranging to travel with a cargo vessel, which will likely take a hefty inducement.

#### **Travel Times**

As a rule of thumb, a traveller on foot or in a horse-drawn wagon can cover around 15 miles in a day, while a traveller mounted on a horse can manage around 30. Express coaches, which change horses along the route, can cover twice that distance, reaching around 60 miles in a day. A river barge travelling downstream can cover roughly 40 miles; while travelling upstream this distance is reduced to a maximum of 15 miles.

These figures assume good roads and reasonable conditions; poorly maintained roads, bad weather, difficult terrain, or other delays can easily reduce the distance covered. When in doubt, use the quality of the road and the circumstances of the journey to adjust these figures rather than worrying about precise calculations.
